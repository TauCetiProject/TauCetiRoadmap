# Roadmap: diophantine approximation and transcendence, Part II — arithmetic algebraization

**Summary.** Build arithmetic coefficient lattices and simultaneous analytic germs, construct multivariable auxiliary functions, and prove that the size m of an independent family is at most e times a positive logarithmic circle mean divided by the logarithm of the uniformizing derivative. Three analytic proofs use a normalized quotient multiplier, successive Jensen averaging, and Vandermonde damping. The resulting algebraicity criterion gives finite-dimensional fields generated by integral holonomic germs after rational simultaneous pole clearing. Centred capacity bounds retain the analytic base point and the scalar field in their statements. The starting point is DiophantineApproximationAndTranscendence §5.1, whose D-finite series and complex initial-value theory supply the differential foundations; the arithmetic algebraization and meromorphic-basis restrictions begin here. (CDT25, Theorem 2.0.1, pp.635–636, CDT25 line 440; §2.5, pp.643–649, CDT25 line 1019.)

## Conventions

All arithmetic statements have characteristic zero. Use ℚ[[x]], ℤ[[t]], ℚ((x)) and ℚ(x), with the native coefficientwise embeddings; ℚ̄ means `AlgebraicClosure ℚ`, with a complex embedding when analytic comparison is needed. Algebraic-integer membership means integrality over ℤ, not membership in a chosen number field. Algebraic series over ℚ̄(x) have their coefficients in a finite number field, as proved in §0.4. Only nonnegative Puiseux series occur, after the finite substitution u=x^(1/N), N>0.

The arithmetic coordinate χ has constant coefficient 0 and linear coefficient 1. It is independent of the analytic map φ. Formal composition means `PowerSeries.subst`; its inner argument has zero constant coefficient, the field case of `PowerSeries.HasSubst`. The fields ℚ(p) and ℚ(x) act inside ℚ((x)); rational functions with poles at zero have Laurent expansions, even though the arithmetic generators are power series. In Lean, a regular rational p is accompanied by a power series pSeries whose Laurent image equals the native image of p. This equality expresses regularity and fixes the Taylor germ, with no arbitrary substitute for p.

A uniformization is an equality of germs at zero, not evaluation of a formal series everywhere on the image of φ. The initial analytic function F need only exist near zero. Global holomorphy of φ and of each uniformized realization on a neighbourhood of the whole closed parameter disc is imposed separately in every closed-disc theorem. Taylor coefficient n is F⁽ⁿ⁾(0)/n!, so x² has coefficient 1 and second derivative 2. For the open-disc criterion, holomorphy is required on the open disc and restriction supplies the closed-disc hypotheses. (CDT25, §1.1.1, p.630, CDT25 line 169; (2.1.1), p.637, CDT25 line 541.)

The parameter circle has Haar mass one; angles t are in turns, with z=exp(2πit). Set λ=|φ′(0)| and require λ>1 wherever log λ is a denominator. Write log⁺r=log(max(1,r)) for r≥0, and J(g)=∫ log⁺|g| dμ. Thus J(2z)=log 2, while the unnormalized arclength integral is 2π log 2. Native totalized log at a zero is used for log|G| only after the zero set is proved null. The Vandermonde product V(z)=∏_(i<j)(z_i−z_j) differs from `Matrix.det_vandermonde` by (−1)^(d(d−1)/2); for (z₁,z₂)=(1,−1) the product is 2 and the native determinant is −2, both of norm 2.

In every auxiliary asymptotic, first α tends to infinity with d,κ fixed, then d tends to infinity, then κ tends to zero; ε tends to zero last in the damping route. The auxiliary estimates hold for d in a cofinal range depending on κ, with a coefficient-height constant independent of both. Natural-number powers include exponent zero; denominators such as d, n! and r are used only with their displayed positivity hypotheses. Empty discrepancy samples have value zero; all sampling inequalities require a nonempty population. (CDT25, Lemma 2.1.2, pp.637–638, CDT25 line 546; §2.5.25, p.648, CDT25 line 1327.)

## How to read the build

Layer 0 separates arithmetic substitution from analytic continuation and supplies denominator bounds. Layer 1 constructs auxiliary functions and compares their integral lowest jets with a multiplier Cauchy estimate. Layer 2 obtains the mean rank bound and algebraicity, and gives the successive-Jensen route. Layer 3 starts again from the same auxiliary construction, adds circular discrepancy and potential-theoretic damping, and recovers the identical bound. Layer 4 imposes the holonomic meromorphic-basis condition, clears poles, bounds W and compares it to V. Layer 5 rescales to centred circles and discs and computes why a translated compact sampling set need not control a germ based at zero. Each route names its own inputs; the Jensen and damping proofs do not depend on the quotient proof's analytic interior.

## Layer 0: arithmetic germs and uniform denominators

### 0.1 Integral coefficient lattices

For a positive integer M and f=Σa_nx^n∈ℚ[[x]], define the integral lattice at scale M by M^n a_n∈ℤ for every n≥0. Equivalently f(Mx) is the coefficientwise image of an integral series. Define `IntegralSeries` to mean the scale-one condition. In an algebraic coefficient field the same lattice uses algebraic integers. For χ=t+Σ_(n≥2)a_nt^n, define the tangent lattice at M by M^(n−1)a_n∈ℤ for n≥2, equivalently χ(Mt)/M∈t+t²ℤ[[t]]. These are predicates on native series, with no additional carrier. (CDT25, §2.1, proof of Lemma 2.1.2, pp.637–638, CDT25 line 574.)

Its API comprises:

- Membership is equivalent to integrality of every M^n coefficient.

- Membership is equivalent to the native rescale M being in the coefficientwise image of integral power series.

- At fixed M the series form a subring; at M=1 it is the image of the native integral-series ring.

- If M divides N and both are positive, membership at M implies membership at N.

- A constant belongs exactly when its value is integral, independently of M.

- For a tangent-to-identity series, χ(Mt)/M is integral exactly when M^(n−1)coeff_nχ is integral for all n≥2.

Use the native coefficientwise map and rescale operation; the lattice is their preimage, not a competing series type. Identify the tangent condition by dividing the rescaled series by the nonzero integer M.

Variable scaling never changes the constant coefficient. The normalized inverse lemma needs the stronger tangent lattice, not just the ordinary scaled lattice. Scaling preserves coefficient zero and hence cannot make a nonintegral constant integral.

**Checks.**

- Σ(x/2)^n belongs at scale 2 and fails at scale 1.

- The constant series 1/2 belongs at no positive integral scale.

- Zero belongs at every positive scale because every scaled coefficient is 0; no nonzero-series condition belongs in the lattice.

- χ=t+t²/4 belongs at ordinary scale 2, but fails the tangent lattice at M=2; M=4 satisfies the latter.

- For f=1+x/2, M=2 gives scaled coefficients (1,1), whereas M=1 leaves 1/2; rescaling cannot repair the constant series 1/2.

- The tangent series χ=t+t² has normalized scale-one coefficients (0,1,1,0,…), all integral; χ=t/2 fails the defining linear coefficient 1 at every normalized scale, since χ(Mt)/M=t/2.

*Needs:* Mathlib `PowerSeries.coeff`; Mathlib `PowerSeries.coeff_rescale`.

### 0.2 Simultaneous analytic uniformization of formal series

Define `Uniformizes` for functions φ,g:ℂ→ℂ and f∈ℚ[[x]] by the existence of F analytic at zero whose nth Taylor coefficient is a_n and whose composite F∘φ has the same germ as g at zero. This predicate alone records a germ identity. Its applications assume φ(0)=0, φ′(0)≠0 and holomorphy of the relevant global representatives on the specified disc. With φ,g holomorphic near the closed unit disc, call g the holomorphic uniformization of f along φ; a finite family has simultaneous uniformization when every member uses that same φ. Define `positiveLogMean` as J(g)=∫_𝕋 log(max(1,|g|))dμ, using the native circle average. Define `rationalFunctionField` of p∈ℚ(x) as the intermediate field generated over ℚ by the Laurent image of p. It fixes the scalar field in every rank statement. (CDT25, §1.1.1, pp.630–631; Theorem 2.0.1, p.635; (2.1.1), p.637, CDT25 line 169.)

Its API comprises:

- The coefficient relation gives F⁽ⁿ⁾(0)/n!=a_n for every n, including n=0; for φ(0)=0 this fixes g(0)=a₀.

- Under φ(0)=0 and φ′(0)≠0, the realization F is unique as a germ; two analytic g with that germ agree on the connected parameter disc.

- The uniformized realizations of 0,1,f+h and fh are 0,1,g_f+g_h and g_fg_h.

- For 0<r≤1, replacing φ(z) by φ(rz) and g by g(rz) preserves the closed-unit-disc relation; the new derivative is rφ′(0). More general radii require holomorphicity on the corresponding larger disc.

- Its Taylor expansion is native substitution of the Taylor series of φ into f; φ(0)=0 is the substitution hypothesis.

- A finite simultaneous family has a common positive radius of convergence in x, obtained from an inverse chart for φ.

- J(constant c)=log(max(1,|c|)), and J(0)=0.

- The generated field contains ℚ and the Laurent image of p and is contained in every intermediate field containing those elements.

- A nonconstant p gives the scalar field ℚ(p); a constant p gives ℚ, with no finite-degree assertion for ℚ(x) over it.

- The field is unchanged when p is replaced by ap+b with a∈ℚ nonzero and b∈ℚ.

- For analytic g near the circle, J is finite, nonnegative and unchanged by a rotation of its argument; J of a constant c is log(max(1,|c|)).

Use analytic germs at zero and Taylor uniqueness; φ′(0)≠0 gives a local inverse and preserves the formal information. The uniformized functions live near the closed disc, while the initial realization F only lives near zero. Use log(max(1,r)) to make the positive logarithm continuous even at g=0.

Specify neighbourhoods of the closed disc, rather than just the open disc, in every finite-radius estimate. For the open-disc André criterion, obtain closed-disc hypotheses by shrinking the parameter disc.

**Checks.**

- For φ(z)=2z, f=x and g(z)=2z, the relation holds and J(g)=log2.

- For m≥2, f=(1−m²x)^(1/m) as its branch with constant 1 and φ(z)=(1−exp(Mz))/m², g(z)=exp(Mz/m) realizes the same germ.

- For f with constant coefficient 1, the negative analytic square-root branch with value −1 at zero does not realize f.

- For f=1/(1−x), analytic continuation is interpreted as a germ identity; a map φ hitting the actual pole 1 cannot have a holomorphic uniformized realization on the closed disc.

- For F(x)=x², the Taylor coefficient at n=2 is 2/2!=1; using the unscaled derivative gives 2 and fails the formal coefficient equality.

- For constant g=2 the positive logarithmic mean is log 2; an arclength integral would give 2π log 2.

- For g=0 the positive logarithmic mean is 0, so the cutoff prevents a logarithmic singularity.

- For constant g=1/2 the positive logarithmic mean is 0, whereas a raw logarithmic mean would be −log 2.

- For p=x, the generated field is ℚ(x) and [ℚ(x):ℚ(p)]=1; taking only the ℚ-linear span of p would fail closure under inversion.

- For p=x², [ℚ(x):ℚ(p)]=2 and x is absent from ℚ(p), distinguishing the smaller scalar field from the ambient field.

- For p=0 or p=2, the generated field is ℚ and the extension ℚ(x)/ℚ is infinite; the nonconstant hypothesis is essential for a finite degree.

*Needs:* Mathlib `PowerSeries.coeff`; Mathlib `PowerSeries.substAlgHom`.

### 0.3 Integral series beyond the unit radius are polynomials

If f=Σ a_n x^n∈ℤ[[x]] has a complex analytic realization on a disc of radius R>1, then a_n=0 for all sufficiently large n; thus f is the image of an integer polynomial. (CDT25, Lemma 1.1.2, p.629, CDT25 line 145.)

Choose 1<r<R and bound the analytic realization on the compact circle of radius r. Cauchy’s coefficient estimate gives |a_n|≤Cr^(−n), eventually less than 1. An integer of absolute value less than 1 vanishes. This is a one-variable Cauchy consequence, not an extra algebraization hypothesis.

The geometric series 1/(1−x) at radius exactly 1 prevents a non-strict radius statement. Polynomials satisfy the conclusion at every finite radius.

**Checks.**

- For f=1+x² the coefficients at n≥3 are zero; at radius R=2 choose r=3/2, obtaining |a_n|≤C(2/3)^n.

- At R=1 the series Σx^n has all coefficients 1 and is not a polynomial, excluding a non-strict radius threshold.

*Needs:* §0.1; §0.2; Mathlib `Complex.norm_iteratedDeriv_le_of_forall_mem_sphere_norm_le`.

### 0.4 Eisenstein denominators for algebraic series

Let y(u)=Σ a_nu^n∈ℚ̄[[u]] be algebraic over ℚ̄(u). There exist positive rational integers c,M such that c M^n a_n is an algebraic integer for every n≥0. If a_0 is an algebraic integer, some positive M′ alone suffices: (M′)^n a_n is integral for every n. The same statement applies to a nonnegative Puiseux series after putting u=x^(1/N), N>0. No variable-only claim is made for a nonintegral constant term. (BB11, §1, p.1, Theorem 6.1, pp.10–11 and §8, p.14, BB11 line 26 and BB11 line 543.)

Write f=y and use the elementary tail-series argument of BB11 §1, p.1. Choose a square-free annihilator P(x,y) over a number field. Since P_y(x,f) is nonzero, let s be its finite order and choose N>s. Write f=b(x)+x^N g with b the truncation below N. The polynomial Q(x,Y)=x^(−N−s)P(x,b(x)+x^N Y) has coefficients in a finite extension containing the finitely many coefficients of b, and Q(0,Y)=aY+b₀ with a≠0. Indeed the constant term is divisible by x^(N+s) because P(x,f)=0, the linear term has exactly that order, and each higher term has order at least 2N>N+s. Include g(0) in that finite extension. After Y=g(0)+u and division by a the equation is u+xR(x,u)=0 for a polynomial R with algebraic coefficients. Choose a positive integer M clearing the finitely many algebraic coefficients of R, the nonconstant coefficients of b and the scalar g(0), using the native integral-multiple theorem. The equation for u(Mt) is u(Mt)=−Mt R(Mt,u(Mt)). Its nth coefficient is an integral polynomial in earlier coefficients, so induction shows u(Mt) has algebraic-integer coefficients. Thus all positive-degree coefficients of f(Mt) are integral after enlarging M if necessary. The same recurrence also places every coefficient in the chosen finite extension. Choose c to clear the remaining constant coefficient. Equivalently, if a first argument gives cM^n a_n integral, an integral a_0 permits replacing M by cM since (cM)^n a_n=c^(n−1)(cM^n a_n) for n≥1. Apply the identical univariate argument after putting u=x^(1/N) for the Puiseux variant.

y=1/2 requires the scalar c; no change of variable alters its constant term. The branch of an inverse rational function used below has zero constant term, so variable-only scaling applies. Only the qualitative denominator estimate is used; quantitative p-adic convergence constants belong to BB11’s stronger theorem.

**Checks.**

- For f=1/2 take c=2,M=1, giving cM^n a_n=(1,0,0,…); M^0a₀ remains 1/2 at every variable scale.

- For f=√(1−4u)=1−2u−2u²−4u³−… take c=M=1; algebraicity is compatible with infinitely many nonzero coefficients.

*Needs:* §0.1; Mathlib `IsAlgebraic.exists_integral_multiple`.

### 0.5 Uniform denominators of a normalized inverse coordinate

Let p∈ℚ(x) be nonconstant and regular at zero, and χ(t)∈t+t²ℚ[[t]] satisfy p(χ(t))∈ℤ[[t]]. There is a positive integer M depending only on p such that χ(Mt)/M∈t+t²ℤ[[t]]. Its formal inverse τ has τ(Mx)/M∈x+x²ℤ[[x]]. Consequently, for every f∈ℚ[[x]], f(χ(t))∈ℤ[[t]] implies f(Mx)∈ℤ[[x]]. One may bound M in terms of the degree and rational coefficient height of p; it is independent of f and of the auxiliary dimension. (CDT25, Proof of Lemma 2.1.2, pp.637–638, CDT25 line 576.)

Write b(p(x)−p(0))/c=x^N+O(x^(N+1)), N>0, with b a nonzero integer and c>0 integral. The normalized inverse Puiseux branch has constant zero. Apply Eisenstein and absorb its scalar. Apply the coefficient estimate (1+u)^(1/N)∈ℤ[[u/N²]] to the normalized Nth root of the integral pullback. The branch with leading term t is selected by χ′(0)=1. The denominator calculation on pp.637–638 supplies M=c²N⁴M₁², with M₁ the scale of that normalized inverse branch. The rescaled series χ(Mt)/M=t+O(t²) has integral coefficients; the native compositional inverse over ℤ has integral coefficients and satisfies both inverse identities. Rescale back to obtain the identical tangent lattice for τ. Compose the integral series f∘χ with τ(Mx), which has integral coefficients and zero constant. For uniform dependence on degree and height, the displayed algebraic construction depends only on p; finitely many rational coefficient lists of bounded degree and height also give a uniform bound.

Both zero constant term and linear coefficient 1 are used to preserve the lowest integral jet.

**Checks.**

- For χ=t+t²/4, ordinary scale 2 gives quadratic coefficient 1; normalized tangent scale 2 gives 1/2, while scale 4 gives 1.

- For p=x², χ=t is admissible and M=1 works despite p′(0)=0; no unramified-coordinate restriction is imposed on p.

- For p=x, the hypotheses already force χ integral, so M=1 works.

- For p=x+x⁴/32, its tangent inverse starts t−t⁴/32+t⁷/256 and satisfies p∘χ=t but is not integral. Tangent scaling M=4 gives coefficients −4³/32=−2 and 4⁶/256=16; the integral series p(4x)/4=x+2x⁴ has integral inverse χ(4t)/4.

## Layer 1: auxiliary functions and arithmetic extrapolation

### 1.1 Independence in separate formal variables

Let p∈ℚ(x) be nonconstant and regular at zero. If f₁,…,f_m∈ℚ[[x]] are linearly independent over ℚ(p(x)), then for every d,D≥1 the (mD)^d series ∏_(s=1)^d p(x_s)^(k_s) f_(i_s)(x_s), with 1≤i_s≤m and 0≤k_s<D, are linearly independent over ℚ in ℚ[[x₁,…,x_d]]. In particular any nonzero coefficient vector gives a nonzero auxiliary function. (CDT25, Proof of Lemma 2.1.2, p.638, CDT25 line 604.)

In one variable, group the powers of p into polynomial coefficients of the f_i. Nonconstancy of p makes powers of p independent over ℚ. For a finite independent family of coefficient sequences choose finitely many coefficient functionals giving an injective map to a finite-dimensional rational space. In d separate variables the corresponding coefficient matrix is a tensor product of injective matrices. Its column independence proves the claim without evaluating an arbitrary formal series at a nonzero rational point.

The scalar field in the hypothesis is ℚ(p); the pair 1,p disproves the version with only ℚ-independence. For f₁=1 the assertion is the independence of separate-variable powers of a nonconstant p.

**Checks.**

- For d=1,D=2,p=x,f₁=1 the series are 1,x, with coefficient matrix [[1,0],[0,1]] and determinant 1.

- For f₁=1,f₂=p,D=2 the entries p·f₁ and 1·f₂ coincide; mere ℚ-independence would give a zero auxiliary sum.

### 1.2 Multivariable auxiliary function with small coefficients

Assume the arithmetic and analytic data of Theorem 2.0.1: nonconstant p∈ℚ(x) regular at 0; χ=t+O(t²) rational; integral p∘χ and f_i∘χ; a common uniformization φ holomorphic near the closed unit disc with φ(0)=0, λ=|φ′(0)|>1, and holomorphic realizations of p and f_i near the closed unit disc; the f_i are ℚ(p)-independent, m≥1. There is C≥0 depending only on p and a fixed inverse-chart radius for φ, independent of d and κ, such that for each 0<κ<1 there is d₁(κ)≥1 and, for every fixed d≥d₁(κ), for all sufficiently large α there are D≥1 and integers a_(i,k), not all zero, for which F=Σ_(i,k) a_(i,k)∏_s p(x_s)^(k_s) f_(i_s)(x_s) is nonzero, has total vanishing order at least α, and satisfies limsup_(α→∞) D/α≤(1+1/κ)^(1/d)/(m(d!)^(1/d)) and limsup_(α→∞) (log max(1,max|a_(i,k)|))/α≤κC. The sums run over i∈{1,…,m}^d and k∈{0,…,D−1}^d. (CDT25, Lemma 2.1.2 and proof, pp.637–638, CDT25 line 546.)

The local inverse chart for φ gives a common convergence disc of radius ρ>0 for the finitely many f_i; choose a smaller closed circle and Cauchy bounds. Inverse-coordinate denominators give M independent of this family. Vanishing of all coefficients of total degree below α gives binomial(α+d−1,d) rational linear equations. Clear them using the common M. On a fixed small inverse-chart circle |x|=r≤1 put P₀=max(1,sup|p|). The integer matrix entries are bounded by exp(α log(M/r)+dD log P₀+o(α)); the fixed family norms and the number of summands contribute o(α) for fixed d. The degree ratio below satisfies dD/α≤e²+o(1) once d≥log(1+1/κ), by d/(d!)^(1/d)≤e. Thus a constant C independent of d,κ controls the matrix on this cofinal dimension range. The factorial bound follows by comparing Σ log j with the integral of log; it holds for every d≥1, including d=1 where 1≤e. Choose D just above the threshold with (mD)^d / binomial(α+d−1,d) tending to 1+1/κ; the row-to-surplus ratio tends to κ. The integral Siegel theorem gives the claimed height estimate. If zero rows occur, discard them or use a coordinate vector. Apply tensor independence to its nonzero kernel vector. Rounding D affects only the o(α) term. All limsups are taken with the remaining parameters fixed.

The total-degree count grows as α^d/d!, and the coefficient-height constant is uniform on d≥d₁(κ). Taking α→∞, then d→∞ and finally κ→0 uses that uniformity; there is no uniform small-κ assertion at every fixed small d.

**Checks.**

- At d=1,κ=1/2,m=1 the limiting D/α threshold is 3 and row-to-surplus ratio is 1/2; omitting surplus would give 1.

### 1.3 Integrality of the lowest nonzero jet

Let F∈ℚ[[x₁,…,x_d]] be nonzero with exact total order β and F(χ(t₁),…,χ(t_d)) integral, where χ=t+O(t²). Its homogeneous degree-β part has integer coefficients. After x_j=φ(z_j), with φ analytic near zero, φ(0)=0 and φ′(0)=a≠0, this part is multiplied by a^β. For D∈ℕ, multiplication by ∏h(z_j)^D with h analytic near zero and h(0)=1 leaves this lowest homogeneous part unchanged. Thus some nonzero coefficient of the resulting series has absolute value at least |a|^β. (CDT25, §2.2, (2.2.1)–(2.2.3), pp.638–639, CDT25 line 620.)

The degree-preserving part of a tangent-to-identity substitution is the identity. All higher substituted terms have larger total degree. The degree-preserving part of φ is scalar multiplication by a in each variable. A monomial of total degree β therefore acquires a^β. Choose a nonzero integer coefficient of the homogeneous part. A factor with constant term 1 cannot change that part.

The coefficient multiplier is a^β, with β the order of F, not the degree of an arbitrary auxiliary polynomial. If χ′(0) is merely nonzero, an extra rational scale appears and the integral lower bound must be changed.

### 1.4 Cauchy extrapolation with a normalized multiplier

Under §1.2's complete arithmetic and analytic hypotheses, let h be holomorphic near the closed unit disc with h(0)=1 and let g uniformize p. Define the multiplier bound as A(h,g)=log(max(sup_𝕋|h|,sup_𝕋|hg|)); equivalently it is the logarithm of the supremum of the pointwise maximum. The boundary compactness gives finite suprema, and the maximum principle gives A≥0. For κ∈(0,1) and d≥d₁(κ), Cauchy extrapolation after α→∞ gives log λ≤[d/(m(d!)^(1/d))](1+1/κ)^(1/d) A+κC. (CDT25, §2.2, (2.2.1)–(2.2.3) and the estimates following them, pp.638–639, CDT25 line 614.)

Its API comprises:

- For holomorphic h,g near the closed disc, A is the logarithm of the larger of their two finite boundary suprema, with hg as the second function.

- If h(0)=1 then A≥0, since sup|h|≥1.

- With h(0)=1, for any real a, A≤a exactly when both boundary norms are at most exp a; for h=1 this gives A=log(max(1,sup|g|)).

Form H(z)=∏h(z_j)^D F(φ(z₁),…,φ(z_d)), holomorphic near the closed unit polydisc. The supplied polydisc Cauchy coefficient formula bounds every coefficient by its boundary supremum. Rewrite each factor h^D g^k as h^(D−k)(hg)^k. The product is bounded by exp(dDA), times the fixed f_i boundary norms. The number of summands is (mD)^d with logarithm o(α); the fixed f_i norms contribute o(α). Compare the integral lower coefficient |a|^β, β≥α, to obtain αlogλ≤dDA+κCα+o(α). Divide and pass to the first limit. The maximum principle and h(0)=1 imply sup|h|≥1, so A≥0 and no logarithm of a nonpositive bound is used.

The Cauchy argument uses holomorphy on a neighbourhood of the compact polydisc and a nonzero lowest jet. For g=h=1, A=0, not 1.

**Checks.**

- For φ=2z,p=x,h=1, g=2z and A=log 2, the limiting right side for m=1 is e log 2, larger than log 2.

- For h=g=1 the boundary maximum is 1 and A=0; inserting a norm instead of its logarithm gives 1 and changes the estimate.

- For h=1+z and g=0, the two boundary suprema are 2 and 0, so A=log 2; the maximum cannot be replaced by the hg supremum alone.

### 1.5 Arithmetic rank bound with a holomorphic multiplier

Under the full arithmetic and analytic hypotheses of §1.2, every holomorphic h near the closed unit disc with h(0)=1 satisfies m≤e A/logλ, with A=log(max(sup_𝕋|h|,sup_𝕋|hg|)) and λ=|φ′(0)|>1. Equivalently A=max(sup log|h|,sup log|hg|), with logarithmic zeros interpreted as −∞ for these suprema. The statement includes the empty family, for which m=0; §1.2 supplies the nonempty case. (CDT25, Lemma 2.0.8, p.636; proof §2.2, pp.638–639, CDT25 line 519.)

For fixed κ, let d→∞ in the Cauchy extrapolation inequality: d/(d!)^(1/d)→e and (1+1/κ)^(1/d)→1. Then let κ decrease to zero, using the independence of C from d and κ. Rearrange using logλ>0. Use the same bound for arbitrary finite families of powers to obtain algebraicity, as detailed in §2.2.

The quantity A is a logarithm of a boundary supremum; zeros of h are permitted. Use log(max of sup norms) or logarithmic suprema; replacing A by a supremum norm changes the theorem. The statement does not require h to have no zeros.

**Checks.**

- For g=2z,h=1−2z, h(0)=1 and h has a zero at 1/2 inside the disc; the two boundary suprema are 3 and 6, so A=log 6 and the bound still applies.

## Layer 2: integrated rank and algebraicity

### 2.1 Integrated arithmetic rank bound

Prove `mean_rank_bound`. Let p∈ℚ(x)\ℚ be regular at 0; χ∈t+t²ℚ[[t]] with p∘χ∈ℤ[[t]]; φ holomorphic on a neighbourhood of the closed unit disc with φ(0)=0, λ=|φ′(0)|>1, and g the holomorphic uniformization of p there. For any m≥0, let f₁,…,f_m∈ℚ[[x]] be ℚ(p)-linearly independent, with f_i∘χ integral and holomorphic uniformized realizations on the same neighbourhood. Then m≤e J(g)/logλ, where J(g)=∫_𝕋 log(max(1,|g|)) dμ_Haar. (CDT25, Theorem 2.0.1 and (2.0.3), p.635, CDT25 line 440; §2.3, Lemma 2.3.1, pp.639–641, CDT25 line 711.)

For every ε>0 import the corrected Nevanlinna quotient representation: a holomorphic h with h(0)=1 has max(sup log|h|,sup log|hg|)≤J(g)+ε. Apply the multiplier bound and let ε decrease to zero. The source’s missing constant phase in reconstruction has no effect on the norm estimates; its supremum must be logarithmic. All arithmetic inputs are unchanged by the choice of h. This proof only imports the quotient theorem, not the rest of punctured-plane uniformization.

The numerator is the normalized mean of log⁺|g|. For p=x, χ=t and φ=Rz with R>1, J=logR and the bound is m≤e. The growth term is a mean of log⁺, not the maximum of |p∘φ| and not an unnormalized arc-length integral.

### 2.2 Algebraicity from a uniform bound on powers

Prove `arithmetic_algebraicity`. Under the scalar hypotheses on p,χ,φ of §2.1, every f∈ℚ[[x]] with f∘χ integral and a holomorphic uniformization u by φ is algebraic over ℚ(x). In fact f is algebraic over ℚ(p(x)). The conclusion does not assert that f is rational or that its analytic continuation is single-valued on the x-plane. (CDT25, End of §2.2, p.639; algebraicity clause of Theorem 2.0.1, p.635, CDT25 line 706.)

If f were transcendental over ℚ(p), every finite list 1,f,…,f^(m−1) would be independent over ℚ(p). All powers retain the integral pullback and their analytic uniformizations are the corresponding powers of u. The right side of the mean rank bound depends only on p,χ,φ, hence is fixed as m increases. Contradict its bound for a sufficiently large m. Since ℚ(p)⊂ℚ(x) and x is algebraic over ℚ(p) for nonconstant rational p, the two algebraicity conclusions are compatible.

Algebraicity is weaker than rationality, including for rational-coefficient series. The binomial examples are nonrational algebraic series. Do not infer rationality from algebraicity of a rational-coefficient formal power series.

**Checks.**

- For f=√(1−4x), f²=1−4x gives a nonzero quadratic annihilator; a conclusion of rationality would be false.

- For f=0 or 1, annihilators Y and Y−1 have degree one; the arbitrary powers proof includes these dependent families.

### 2.3 André’s open-disc algebraicity criterion

Let f∈ℤ[[x]]. Suppose φ is holomorphic on the open unit disc, φ(0)=0 and |φ′(0)|>1, and the formal pullback has a holomorphic realization on that disc in the germ sense of §0.2. Then f is algebraic over ℚ(x). (CDT25, §1.1.1, pp.630–631; derived from Theorem 2.0.1, CDT25 line 169.)

Choose r<1 so close to 1 that r|φ′(0)|>1. The maps φ(rz) and the corresponding uniformized f are holomorphic on a neighbourhood of the closed unit disc. Use p=x, χ=t; the integral pullback of p is t. Apply arithmetic algebraicity. The argument derives this open-disc theorem directly from the closed-disc rank theorem.

CDT25 (1.1.4)–(1.1.5), p.631, supplies the named boundary benchmark F(x)=Σ binomial(2n,n)²x^n: it is integral and transcendental, while φ(q)=λ(q)/16 has φ′(0)=1 and F∘φ=(Σ_(n∈ℤ)q^(n²))² on |q|<1. Thus equality at the derivative threshold cannot imply algebraicity. These modular identity data are source benchmark inputs, not a second development of modular functions here. The numerical derivative threshold is not the radius of convergence of f in x; simultaneous uniformization can cross the original convergence circle.

### 2.4 A logarithmic estimate for the first lexicographic coefficient

For d≥1 let G be holomorphic on a neighbourhood of the closed unit polydisc with nonzero Taylor germ at zero. Let c z₁^(n₁)⋯z_d^(n_d), c≠0, be the lexicographically least monomial of its Taylor support, in the coordinate order z₁,…,z_d. Then log|G| is integrable on the unit polycircle after arbitrary finite values on its zero set, that zero set is Haar-null, and log|c|≤∫_(𝕋^d) log|G| dμ. This least monomial need not have the least total degree. (CDT25, Lemma 2.4.2, (2.4.3)–(2.4.5), p.642, CDT25 line 936.)

For d=1 factor out the exact zero order and apply Jensen to the remaining function with nonzero central value. Its isolated circle zeros are integrable logarithmic singularities. For d>1 factor z₁^(n₁), and put H(0,z′) equal to the first nonzero coefficient function. Apply the one-variable Jensen inequality to almost every slice; exceptional slices on which it vanishes identically are contained in the zero set of that nonzero coefficient function. Apply induction to H(0,z′). For Fubini, first use the uniform upper bound log|G|≤log sup|G| and the integrable lower bound coming from the coefficient function. This controls the negative part and justifies the iterated integral; do not assign a genuine −∞ singularity the value zero on a positive-measure set. Integrate the slice inequalities. The logarithm can use the native totalized value at zeros only after their Haar-nullness has been proved.

Lexicographic least exponent and least total degree are different notions. For G=c z^n the inequality is equality. For G=z₂^100+z₁, the least lexicographic monomial is z₂^100 while the least total degree is 1; the proof must not confuse them.

- For G=z₂^100+z₁ the least lexicographic exponent is (0,100), not the least total-degree exponent (1,0).

## Layer 3: circle discrepancy and Vandermonde damping

### 3.1 Circular box discrepancy

For d≥1 and an indexed tuple z₁,…,z_d on 𝕋, define circular discrepancy, denoted D_circ(z)=sup_(a∈ℝ,0≤ℓ≤1)|d^(−1)#{j:z_j∈I(a,ℓ)}−ℓ|, where I(a,ℓ)={exp(2πit): frac(t−a)∈[0,ℓ)}; I(a,0) is empty and I(a,1)=𝕋. Points are counted with multiplicity. Equivalently take the supremum over cuts a of the supplied star discrepancy of the rotated arguments frac(arg(z_j)/(2π)−a). On a Borel probability ν on 𝕋 use the same arc formula with ν(I) in place of the empirical frequency. This is an adapter to the owner’s star discrepancy, not a second general discrepancy library. Assign value zero to the empty tuple separately; every sampling inequality below assumes d≥1. (CDT25, Definition 2.5.4, p.644; adaptation of the existing ProbabilisticAndMetricNumberTheory §2.7 star-discrepancy node, CDT25 line 1082.)

Its API comprises:

- The value is the supremum over the half-open arcs I(a,ℓ), with Haar mass ℓ.

- It equals sup_a D*(frac(θ_j−a)); only the angle adapter is new.

- For d≥1, 1/d≤D_circ≤1; empirical equality to Haar is impossible.

- A permutation of the indices preserves the value, retaining all repeated points.

- Multiplication of every point by a common unit scalar preserves the value.

- Repeating every entry exactly r≥1 times leaves the discrepancy unchanged.

- The tuple value equals the measure version evaluated at the supplied finite empirical probability measure.

- The measure discrepancy of normalized Haar is zero, and zero discrepancy characterizes Haar.

Use the native unit circle or its additive angle coordinate, with their standard exponential identification. Every proper half-open circular arc is one initial interval after a rotation of the cut. Define the supremum from empirical frequencies or from the supremum of rotated star discrepancies, and prove the two agree. Arc length ℓ is normalized Haar mass. Keep d>0 for a tuple; the owner’s empty-sample convention is not used in the damping theorem.

The lower bound 1/d≤D_circ≤1 holds for nonempty d-tuples. Changing half-open endpoint convention leaves the supremum unchanged, although individual arc counts can change.

**Checks.**

- A singleton has discrepancy 1.

- The dth roots of unity, once each, have discrepancy 1/d.

- Any positive number of identical points has discrepancy 1; the empirical law remains a point mass.

- An antipodal pair has discrepancy 1/2, at every rotation.

- The tuple (1,1,−1) has discrepancy 2/3, whereas its set of distinct values gives the antipodal probability with discrepancy 1/2; discarding unequal multiplicities changes the object.

- The empty population is assigned discrepancy 0 separately; the raw expression with division by d=0 would have supremum 1.

*Needs:* ProbabilisticAndMetricNumberTheory §2.7; Tau Ceti `TauCeti.Probability.empiricalMeasureOfFintype`.

### 3.2 Weak limits and circular discrepancy

The measure discrepancy ν↦D_circ(ν) is continuous on Borel probability measures on 𝕋 for weak convergence. In particular, if empirical probabilities converge weakly to Haar, their circular discrepancies tend to zero; if their discrepancies are bounded below by ε>0, any weak limit differs from Haar. (CDT25, Proof of Lemma 2.5.9, p.646, the discrepancy passage; detailed finite-mesh justification, CDT25 line 1161.)

For a fixed limit measure ν choose a finite circular mesh with arbitrarily small Haar length between consecutive endpoints and with all endpoints outside the at most countably many atoms of ν. Weak convergence gives convergence of the finitely many mesh-arc masses, since their boundaries are ν-null. This is the usual Portmanteau consequence of the native bounded-continuous-test characterization. Enclose or inscribe any arc by mesh arcs. Monotonicity of every probability measure controls its mass, while the Haar error of enlarging each endpoint is at most the mesh length. Thus both discrepancies differ from the finite mesh maximum by a uniformly small error. Pass first to the weak limit and then refine the mesh. This argument works even when the limit has atoms; it does not assert uniform convergence of all distribution functions.

Weak continuity of the scalar discrepancy does not assert uniform convergence of distribution functions at atoms. Weak convergence of two nearby point masses to one point mass leaves discrepancy tending to 1. A regular d-gon’s empirical measures converge to Haar and its discrepancy 1/d tends to zero.

### 3.6 Fekete’s extremal Vandermonde on the circle

For d≥1 and z_j∈𝕋, let V(z)=∏_(i<j)(z_i−z_j). Then |V(z)|≤d^(d/2), with equality precisely when the tuple, up to permutation, is a rotation of all dth roots of unity. Apply SuccessiveMinima §0.1, target 0.1.1, to the native Vandermonde rows, each of Euclidean norm √d; dependent rows give determinant zero. This supplies the upper bound without constructing another general Hadamard theorem. For equality, inspect the Gram–Schmidt norm factors: equality forces the rows to be pairwise orthogonal. Thus every distinct ratio is a nontrivial dth root of unity. Fixing one row and using distinctness gives every vertex of a rotated regular d-gon, and the converse follows from the same finite geometric sums.

The determinant identity fixes the sign; its modulus bound and polygon equality require the norm argument. d=2 gives |z₁−z₂|≤2 with equality at antipodal pairs.

### 3.7 Haar equilibrium and the circle energy gap

### 3.8 Exponential Vandermonde loss away from Haar

For each ε∈(0,1] there are c_ε>0 and d₀≥1 such that if d≥d₀ and z∈𝕋^d has D_circ(z)≥ε, then |V(z)|<exp(−c_ε d²). The assertion allows collisions and requires no explicit dependence of c_ε,d₀ on ε. (CDT25, Lemma 2.5.9 and proof, pp.645–646, CDT25 line 1134.)

Collisions already give the desired conclusion and can be excluded from the contrary sequence. Compactness of probability measures supported on the compact metric circle gives a weakly convergent subsequence ν_d→ν; sequential extraction follows from metrizability. Discrepancy continuity shows ν≠Haar. For A>0 use the continuous bounded kernel k_A(z,w)=min(A,log(1/|z−w|)), with k_A(z,z)=A. ## Layer 4: holonomic rings and scalar spans

### 4.1 Integral holonomic germs with meromorphic local bases

Let U⊆ℂ be open with 0∈U and χ∈t+t²ℚ[[t]]. Define H(U,χ,ℤ) to be the subset of ℚ[[x]] consisting of f with f∘χ∈ℤ[[t]] for which some nonzero linear differential operator L∈ℚ̄(x)[∂] annihilates f and has a full meromorphic local solution basis at every singular point in U. At an order-n singularity this means n independent solutions meromorphic on a whole neighbourhood, spanning the punctured-neighbourhood solution space. The zero series is included. The existence of a suitable operator, rather than a chosen operator or the minimal operator, is part of membership. (CDT25, Definition 2.0.4 and its local-basis convention, pp.635–636, CDT25 line 467.)

The intended construction is a subring of the native power-series ring, indexed by U and χ; it is defined by this membership condition and the closure in §4.2, not by arbitrary extra operations. Positive-order annihilators may be used without loss; zero is annihilated by differentiation, and all ordinary-point solution spaces have their full order dimension.

Its API comprises:

- Membership is exactly integral pullback plus the existence of an algebraic-coefficient annihilator with the stated full local bases.

- Zero and one belong for every U and χ.

- H is closed under addition, negation and multiplication by the closure theorem.

- For open U′⊆U containing 0, H(U,χ,ℤ)⊆H(U′,χ,ℤ).

- For χ=t the ring is a ℤ[x]-algebra; when p∈ℚ(x) is regular at 0 and p∘χ is integral it is a ℤ[p]-algebra.

- Membership implies D-finiteness over ℚ̄ and, because f has rational coefficients, D-finiteness over ℚ by the coefficient descent proved in this subsection.

Intersect the integral-pullback predicate with existence of the imported differential equation and its full meromorphic-basis property. Keep the local condition at every singularity in U, including the initial point when it is singular; the series f itself has no negative powers at zero. Use the closure theorem below to supply the subring structure.

For every f∈ℚ[[x]], a nonzero ℚ̄[x]-coefficient annihilator of order n and coefficient degrees at most d yields a nonzero ℚ[x]-coefficient annihilator of the same order and degree bound. This coefficient descent is a target here. Clear rational-function denominators, take a ℚ-basis of the finite number field generated by the operator coefficients, and expand each polynomial in that basis. Since every derivative of f has rational coefficients, each basis component annihilates f. At least one component of the leading polynomial is nonzero; choosing it preserves the order. This is a linear-algebra consequence of the annihilator condition in Definition 2.0.4, rather than an additional local-basis assertion. (CDT25, Definition 2.0.4, p.635, CDT25 line 467.)

A pole is allowed for other local solutions; it is only the chosen formal f that must be regular at zero.

**Checks.**

- The series 1 belongs even for U=ℂ: its pullback coefficients are (1,0,0,…) and y′=0 has the full basis {1}, excluding a zero-only arithmetic ring.

- For χ=t, f=1/(1−x) belongs on ℂ: (1−x)f′−f=0 has rational coefficients and the full meromorphic basis {1/(1−x)} at 1. Multiplying this operator by √2 and expanding in the basis (1,√2) recovers that same rational annihilator.

- For f=exp(x), an order-one constant-coefficient annihilator a₀f+a₁f′=0 imposes a₀+a₁=0: the coefficient matrix has rank 1 and kernel spanned by (−1,1) over both ℚ and ℚ̄. Nevertheless f fails integral coefficients for χ=t, since its x² coefficient is 1/2.

- Put L(t)=−log(1−t), χ=L/(1+L), and f(x)=exp(x/(1−x)). Then χ=t+O(t²) is rational-coefficient and f∘χ=1/(1−t) is integral. The rational-coefficient germ f is D-finite, satisfying (1−x)²f′−f=0, but f does not belong to H(ℂ,χ,ℤ): its essential singularity at 1 precludes every full-meromorphic-basis annihilator. Thus the local condition is independent of D-finiteness and integral pullback.

### 4.2 Ring closure and rational multipliers

For U,χ as above, H(U,χ,ℤ) is a subring of ℚ[[x]]. If p∈ℚ(x) is regular at 0 (as the integrality of p∘χ presupposes) and p∘χ is integral, then multiplication by p preserves H, whether or not p has poles in U; hence it is a ℤ[p]-algebra. For χ=t, multiplication by x preserves H and it is a ℤ[x]-algebra. (CDT25, Ring and module assertions following Definition 2.0.4, pp.635–636; rational differential-module closure spells out the property used there, CDT25 line 473.)

Integral pullbacks are closed under the ring operations. Part I §5.1 supplies finite-dimensional derivative spans. Here form the direct sum and tensor product of the finite-dimensional ℚ̄(x) differential modules generated by f and g. Take the cyclic submodules generated by f⊕g and f⊗g, respectively, and obtain scalar annihilators of f+g and fg from linear dependence of successive derivatives. Tracking full meromorphic local bases through these constructions is part of this closure target. On a punctured neighbourhood the input differential modules have full horizontal meromorphic bases. Their direct sums and tensors do too. The cyclic differential subquotient defining the resulting scalar equation inherits this property: a horizontal frame identifies these modules and their subquotients with constant finite-dimensional spaces. This also covers any new apparent singularities of the cyclic presentation. A nonzero rational p spans the solution space of py′−p′y=0, which has the meromorphic basis p at every zero and pole of p. Replacing L by y↦L(p⁻¹y) (equivalently, tensoring with this rank-one module) gives an annihilator of pf with coefficients in ℚ̄(x) and leading coefficient a_n/p≠0; its local solution bases are p times those of L, hence meromorphic at every singular point in U, including the zeros and poles of p. The case p=0 is trivial. Regularity of p at 0 keeps p∘χ and pf inside formal power series, and (pf)∘χ=(p∘χ)(f∘χ) is integral. For χ=t, x has integral pullback and is regular everywhere.

The derivative need not preserve the arithmetic ring for arbitrary χ; no derivative-closure API is asserted. An arbitrary rational multiplier can destroy integral coefficients and is available only after taking the scalar spans.

**Checks.**

- For χ=t, multiplying 1 by x gives x∈H; multiplying 1 by the rational constant 1/2 gives nonintegral constant coefficient 1/2.

- For χ=t and f=g=1/(1−x), write D=d/dx. The sum 2/(1−x) has annihilator (1−x)D−1 and the product (1−x)⁻² has annihilator (1−x)D−2. Their full local bases have pole orders 1 and 2 at 1; a construction retaining only the selected holomorphic germ would miss these poles.

- For U=ℂ, χ=t and p=1/(1−x), p∘χ=Σtⁿ is integral and p·1=1/(1−x) belongs to H(ℂ,t,ℤ) with annihilator (1−x)D−1 and full basis {1/(1−x)} at 1, although p has a pole at 1∈U; requiring p to be regular on U would wrongly exclude it. Regularity on U is a hypothesis only of §4.3 and the rank theorems built on it, where p∘φ must be holomorphic.

- For f=1 and g=−1, f+g=0 admits the order-zero annihilator 1 and its solution space has dimension 0. The cyclic module generated by (1,−1) instead has dimension 1 and annihilator D; passage to the sum can lower order.

- For χ=t and f=g=x, the product x² satisfies (xD−2)y=0, with full meromorphic basis {x²} at 0. Its value there is 0, so invertibility of evaluation at the singular point cannot replace the meromorphic-basis condition.

### 4.3 Two distinct scalar spans of integral holonomic germs

Assume the data of H and let p∈ℚ(x) be nonconstant, regular on U, with p∘χ integral. Embed ℚ[[x]] and ℚ(x) in ℚ((x)). Set W(U,χ,p)=span_(ℚ(p)) H(U,χ,ℤ), and V(U,χ,ℤ)=span_(ℚ(x)) H(U,χ,ℤ), both inside this same Laurent-series field. Then W⊆V and V=ℚ(x)W. The space V is the source’s V; W is the smaller span to which the mean rank argument directly applies. Neither space is defined as a span over ℚ alone. (CDT25, Definition 2.0.4, p.635; correction of the scalar-span passage in Corollary 2.0.5, p.636, CDT25 line 473.)

- W elements are finite ℚ(p)-linear combinations of H elements.

- V elements are finite ℚ(x)-linear combinations of H elements.

- W and V are subalgebras over their displayed fields and contain their scalar fields via 1∈H.

- V=ℚ(x)W inside ℚ((x)); restriction of scalars embeds W in V.

- If χ is integral, x∈H, and after the finite-rank theorem W is a field containing ℚ(x), hence W=V.

- For χ=t, V agrees exactly with the source’s V(U,ℤ); the parent D-finite predicate is a condition on generators, not on an arbitrary chosen presentation.

Use native scalar restriction and submodule spans in the common Laurent-series field; rational functions are interpreted through their Laurent expansion at zero. Expand each finite span explicitly. Closure of H under multiplication makes both spans subalgebras over their respective scalar fields, containing 1. The inclusions of scalar fields and the iterated finite-span identity give W⊆V and V=ℚ(x)W.

The scalar field in a dimension statement is part of its type; x need not belong to W before finiteness when χ is nonintegral.

**Checks.**

- For every admissible datum, ℚ(p)⊆W and ℚ(x)⊆V, so neither space has rank zero.

- For p=x and χ=t the scalar fields coincide and W=V by definition.

- For p=x+x⁴/32 and χ its tangent inverse, V contains ℚ(x), whose degree over ℚ(p) is 4. This cannot be bounded as though V were just the span over ℚ(p).

- Even though generators are power series, x⁻¹ belongs to V. The ambient space must therefore allow Laurent expansions.

- For p=x the generated field ℚ(p) is ℚ(x); for p=x², the degree of ℚ(x) over ℚ(p) is 2; for constant p=0 it is infinite and the nonconstant hypothesis fails.

*Needs:* §4.1; §4.2; Mathlib `RatFunc.coeToLaurentSeries`.

### 4.4 Uniformization after rational simultaneous pole clearing

For a finite family f_i∈H(U,χ,ℤ), a nonconstant p∈ℚ(x) without poles on U with p∘χ integral, and φ holomorphic near the closed unit disc with image in U, φ(0)=0 and φ′(0)≠0, there is a nonzero q∈ℤ[t] such that every q(p)f_i has integral χ-pullback and a holomorphic uniformization on a neighbourhood of that closed disc. Multiplication by this common nonzero factor preserves ℚ(p)-linear independence. (CDT25, Proof of Corollary 2.0.5, p.636, CDT25 line 502.)

Each algebraic-coefficient differential operator has finitely many algebraic singularities; take their union for the chosen finite family. Import rational simultaneous pole clearing using sufficiently high powers of the minimal polynomials of p(α), at singularities α lying in U. Multiply the polynomial by an integer to make all its coefficients integral. Every local solution multiplied by q(p) is holomorphic throughout U at the formerly singular points. Continue the initial germ along paths pulled back to the parameter disc. The imported uniqueness and monodromy argument on a slightly larger simply connected disc give single-valued holomorphic realizations. A sufficiently small enlargement still maps into U by compactness. The factor q(p∘χ) is integral. Since p is nonconstant, q(p)≠0; multiplication is injective in the Laurent-series field and preserves independence. No Galois invariance of the subset of singularities lying in U is assumed: rational minimal polynomials may add harmless conjugate zeros.

The common factor is rational integral and clears full local bases, including their poles. A polynomial with complex coefficients would not preserve the arithmetic pullback condition. Pole clearing is applied to the full local bases, rather than only a selected branch.

**Checks.**

- For the solution f=1/(1−x), p=x and χ=t, q(t)=1−t gives q(p)f=1; q=i(1−t) would give constant i and leave ℚ[[t]].

- At α=1/√2, p=x, χ=t and f=1/(1−2x²), the integral rational q=1−2t² clears both α and its conjugate, although a selected domain need contain only α.

*Needs:* §4.1; §4.2; ConformalMappingPartII §0.6; ConformalMappingPartII §0.3; ConformalMappingPartII §0.4; §0.2.

### 4.5 Corrected holonomic rank bound

Let U⊆ℂ be open with 0∈U; χ=t+O(t²)∈ℚ[[t]]; p∈ℚ(x) nonconstant without poles on U with p∘χ integral. Let φ be holomorphic near the closed unit disc, map it and a neighbourhood into U, satisfy φ(0)=0, and have λ=|φ′(0)|>1. Then W(U,χ,p) has finite dimension over ℚ(p), and dim_(ℚ(p))W≤B:=eJ(p∘φ)/logλ. This bound is for W as defined above, without adjoining all rational functions in x. (CDT25, Corollary 2.0.5 and its proof, p.636, with the scalar span interpreted on W as in §4.6, CDT25 line 489.)

Every finite independent family in W can be replaced by an equally large independent family of H generators: choose generators in finite span presentations and perform finite-dimensional basis extraction. Apply simultaneous integral pole clearing to this family. Its independence and integral pullbacks survive, and its uniformizations are holomorphic. Apply the mean rank theorem. A uniform real bound on all finite independent families implies finite dimension and the dimension bound.

The estimate controls W; V over ℚ(p) requires the scalar-span comparison. The derivative and logarithmic mean have the same normalization as in Layer 2. The printed bound on dim_(ℚ(p))V is not a consequence unless W=V or another scalar-extension factor is included.

**Checks.**

- For U=ℂ,p=x,χ=t,φ=2z the rank bound gives dim W≤e, hence dim W≤2 because dimension is integral; 1∈W gives dim W≥1.

- For λ=1 the denominator is zero; the strict map-size hypothesis survives pole clearing because φ is unchanged.

*Needs:* §4.3; §4.4; §2.1.

### 4.6 Field structure and safe bounds for V

Under §4.5, W and V are fields in ℚ((x)), finite over ℚ(p) and ℚ(x), respectively. Write n=[ℚ(x):ℚ(p)]<∞. Then dim_(ℚ(x))V≤dim_(ℚ(p))W≤B and dim_(ℚ(p))V≤n·B. If χ∈ℤ[[t]], then W=V, so the stronger bound dim_(ℚ(p))V≤B holds. In all cases V is finite-dimensional over ℚ(x). (CDT25, Corrected interpretation of Corollary 2.0.5, pp.635–636; finite-field argument fills the passage from the bounded generator span to V, CDT25 line 489.)

Apply Mathlib’s `fieldOfFiniteDimensional` to W as a finite-dimensional domain over ℚ(p). Its inverse agrees with the ambient Laurent inverse because multiplication by a nonzero element is injective. A ℚ(p)-basis of W spans V over ℚ(x), giving its dimension bound and then its field structure by the same native construction. Import the native rational-function extension-degree formula: for nonconstant p=a/b in reduced form, [ℚ(x):ℚ(p)]=max(deg a,deg b)<∞. The scalar tower formula gives the second displayed inequality. If χ is integral then x∈H. The field W consequently contains ℚ(x), so V=ℚ(x)W=W.

Multiplication by n is indispensable in the general χ statement about dimension over ℚ(p). The printed first, qualitative finiteness claim is preserved.

**Checks.**

- For p=x² and integral χ=t on U=ℂ, x∈H and W=V; the scalar subfield degree is 2, illustrating that W=V does not mean ℚ(p)=ℚ(x).

- A one-dimensional field subalgebra has rank 1; the zero ring cannot occur since 1 is the nonzero Laurent series 1.

*Needs:* §4.5; §4.2; §4.3; Mathlib `RatFunc.finrank_eq_max_natDegree`; Mathlib `fieldOfFiniteDimensional`.

### 4.7 Finiteness for an integral coordinate above radius one

Let U⊆ℂ be open with 0∈U. If there is a holomorphic map φ from the open unit disc into U with φ(0)=0 and |φ′(0)|>1, then V(U,ℤ)=V(U,t,ℤ) is finite-dimensional over ℚ(x). More generally the same conclusion holds for integral χ=t+O(t²). For a connected hyperbolic U the hypothesis is equivalent to conformal radius at 0 greater than 1; for U=ℂ or ℂ∖{a} with a≠0 use the explicit disc-map hypothesis rather than a hyperbolic-radius definition outside its domain. (CDT25, First assertion of Corollary 2.0.5, p.636, CDT25 line 489.)

Choose 1/|φ′(0)|<r<1 and restrict to φ(rz). This supplies a neighbourhood of the closed disc with derivative r|φ′(0)|>1. Take p=x, whose χ-pullback is integral. The corrected rank theorem bounds W=V directly. For connected hyperbolic U import the conformal-radius universal-cover characterization. For other open U the operational disc-map condition suffices and requests no new general uniformization theorem.

The map criterion has a strict derivative bound and is meaningful on nonhyperbolic domains. A map with derivative exactly 1 does not meet this sufficient criterion. For U=ℂ, φ(z)=2z supplies the hypothesis; the formulation does not try to evaluate a hyperbolic conformal radius at a parabolic surface.

**Checks.**

- For U=ℂ,χ=t and φ=2z the hypothesis holds; for U=𝔻 every based disc map has derivative modulus at most 1, so this sufficient criterion supplies no conclusion.

- For U=ℂ∖{1}, φ=1−exp(2z) avoids 1, fixes zero and has derivative −2; no hyperbolic radius is assigned to this parabolic domain.

*Needs:* §4.6; ConformalMappingPartII §1.3; ConformalMappingPartII §1.4; §0.2.

### 4.8 Counterexample to the unrestricted V rank bound

In the published Corollary 2.0.5, take U=ℂ, p=x+x⁴/32, χ the formal inverse of p tangent to the identity, and φ(z)=2z. Then p∘χ=t∈ℤ[[t]] and all printed hypotheses hold. Since 1∈H, dim_(ℚ(p))V≥[ℚ(x):ℚ(p)]=4. However J(p∘φ)≤log(5/2), so eJ(p∘φ)/log2<4. Thus the printed (2.0.7) for V is false for general rational χ. (CDT25, Definition 2.0.4, pp.635–636; Corollary 2.0.5, (2.0.7), p.636, CDT25 line 492.)

The native formal inverse exists because p(0)=0 and p′(0)=1; both compositions are the identity. Its fourth coefficient is −1/32, so χ is rational but nonintegral. On |z|=1, |p(2z)|=|2z+z⁴/2|≤5/2. Hence its positive-log average is at most log(5/2). The polynomial p has degree 4, giving the extension degree 4. Finally e<3 and (5/2)³=125/8<16=2⁴ show e log(5/2)/log2<4. All points of U are allowed because p is a polynomial.

The degree-four counterexample concerns scalar extension; it does not contradict the integrated rank theorem. Taking p=x would conceal this problem because its scalar fields coincide. The corrected W bound and the integral-coordinate bound survive this example.

**Checks.**

- The tangent inverse begins χ=t−t⁴/32+t⁷/256+O(t¹⁰); p∘χ=t while coefficient 4 is −1/32. The coefficients at degrees 4 and 7 in both p∘χ and χ∘p are 0: −1/32+1/32=0 and 1/256−4/32²=0.

- The boundary bound is 5/2; (5/2)³=125/8<16=2⁴ and e<3 give B<4, whereas the scalar extension contributes dimension at least 4.

*Needs:* §4.3; §4.6; §2.1; Mathlib `RatFunc.finrank_eq_max_natDegree`; Mathlib `PowerSeries.substInv`; Mathlib `PowerSeries.subst_substInv_right`; Mathlib `PowerSeries.subst_substInv_left`.

## Layer 5: centred capacity bounds and the compact-set obstruction

### 5.1 Capacity normalization on centred circles and discs

Assume U,χ,p as in §4.5, and let φ be holomorphic on a neighbourhood of the closed disc |z|≤r, r>0, with image there in U, φ(0)=0, and r|φ′(0)|>1. For K={|z|≤r} or K={|z|=r}, its capacity is r and its equilibrium measure is normalized arc measure on the boundary circle. Then dim_(ℚ(p))W≤e∫_K log⁺|p∘φ|dμ_K/(log|φ′(0)|+log cap(K)). The same bound holds for dim_(ℚ(x))V, and for dim_(ℚ(p))V if χ is integral. For general χ the latter bound requires the factor [ℚ(x):ℚ(p)]. (CDT25, Valid centred-disc and circle cases of (2.5.28), p.649; proof by rescaling Corollary 2.0.5, CDT25 line 1364.)

Apply §4.5 to ψ(z)=φ(rz). Its derivative modulus is r|φ′(0)| and its circle integral is the displayed equilibrium integral. Import capacity of the disc and circle as r and the uniqueness of their rotationally invariant equilibrium probability. This proves the formula exactly for both centred shapes, including r≠1. Use the scalar-span comparison for the asserted V versions.

The denominator must be positive, not merely nonzero. For r=1 this recovers the corrected circle corollary. The conformal centre is fixed at 0; translation of K while leaving φ(0)=0 fixed is not a consequence.

**Checks.**

- For r=1/2 and φ=4z, the derivative product is 2, the denominator log 2, J on the radius-r boundary is log 2, and B=e.

- For r=1/2 and φ=2z, the derivative product is 1 and the denominator 0, so the theorem does not assert a bound.

*Needs:* §4.5; §4.6; ClassicalArithmeticCompletion §§6.18,6.40 and the energy/equilibrium supplier contract.

### 5.2 Failure of the arbitrary-compact capacity formula

Let K be the closed disc with centre 1/2 and radius 1/4, contained in the open unit disc. Take U=ℂ, p=x, χ=t and φ(z)=8z exp(−20z). Then φ(0)=0, |φ′(0)|=8, cap(K)=1/4 and log|φ′(0)|+log cap(K)=log2>0. Yet ∫_K log⁺|p∘φ|dμ_K=0 while dim_(ℚ(x))V≥1. Thus the arbitrary-compact claim (2.5.28) fails even with integral χ and identical scalar fields. (CDT25, (2.5.28) and the paragraph introducing K, p.649, CDT25 line 1364.)

For every z∈K, Re z≥1/4 and |z|≤3/4, hence |φ(z)|≤6exp(−5)<1. The integrand vanishes pointwise on K, so its integral is zero for the supplied equilibrium probability. Translation invariance and the disc capacity value give cap K=1/4. Since 8·(1/4)=2, the denominator is log 2>0. The constant germ 1 lies in H and gives a nonzero vector in W=V, so the proposed rank bound would force 1≤0. This counterexample specifies the obstruction to a general compact-set extension: an admissible replacement must connect its sampling set to the distinguished analytic base point. The positive centred statement is §5.1.

The compactum has positive capacity and the denominator is strictly positive, so neither degeneracy explains the failure. The map is entire and p has no poles anywhere; shrinking away from singularities cannot repair this example.

**Checks.**

- On K=closedDisk(1/2,1/4), Re z≥1/4 and |z|≤3/4 give |8z exp(−20z)|≤6exp(−5)<1; the numerator is 0.

- The denominator is log 8+log(1/4)=log 2>0 while 1∈V gives rank≥1; the proposed inequality would read 1≤0.

*Needs:* §4.3; §5.1; ClassicalArithmeticCompletion §§6.18,6.40 and the energy/equilibrium supplier contract.

### Examples

For r=1/2 and φ=4z, cap K=1/2, |φ′(0)|=4 and the denominator is log 2; the boundary value has modulus 2, so the bound is e. For K=closedDisk(1/2,1/4) and φ=8z exp(−20z), capacity is 1/4 and the denominator remains log 2, but |φ|≤6exp(−5)<1 throughout K and the numerator is zero. Its rank is at least one. The translated sampling set therefore cannot be substituted into the centred result. (CDT25, (2.5.28), p.649, CDT25 line 1364; Saff10, Example 1.10, p.173, Saff10 line 387.)

## Worked examples

For the nonintegral coordinate χ inverse to x+x⁴/32, one has p∘χ=t and χ=t−t⁴/32+O(t⁷). With φ=2z, |p(2z)|≤5/2 on the unit circle. The scalar degree is four, but B≤e log(5/2)/log 2<4. (CDT25, Definition 2.0.4 and (2.0.7), pp.635–636, CDT25 line 467; the calculation is §4.8.)

The strict derivative threshold has two boundary benchmarks. The lacunary integral series Σ_(k≥0)x^(2^k) converges on the unit disc and has singularities at every dyadic root of unity: along a radial approach the tail contains arbitrarily many terms tending to 1. These roots are dense, whereas an algebraic function has only finitely many possible singularities. The identity parameter has derivative 1 and therefore fails the criterion's strict premise. CDT25's modular benchmark is F(x)=Σ binomial(2n,n)²x^n, with φ(q)=λ(q)/16=q−8q²+44q³+…, derivative 1, and F∘φ=(Σ_(n∈ℤ)q^(n²))². The first two nonconstant coefficients are 4 and 4: 4·1=4 and 4·(−8)+36=4; this fixes the factor 16 and the theta square. Its transcendence and modular identity are benchmark data from the source. (CDT25, (1.1.4)–(1.1.5), p.631, CDT25 line 242.)
