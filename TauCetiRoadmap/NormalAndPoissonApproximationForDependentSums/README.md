# Roadmap: normal and Poisson approximation for sums with local dependence or couplings

**Summary.** A sum of many weakly dependent random variables is close to a normal law, and a sum
of many rare, weakly dependent indicators is close to a Poisson law. This roadmap proves explicit
bounds of this kind by Stein's method under local dependence and under
couplings, and applies them to random graphs and coin tossing. Distances are the Wasserstein
distance `W₁` on the normal side and Mathlib's total variation distance, twice the one of many
texts, on the Poisson side.

Local dependence is described by a dependency graph: symmetric neighbourhoods `Nᵢ ∋ i` such that
two subfamilies with no index of one in the neighbourhood of an index of the other are
independent. The first headline theorem: if the `Xᵢ`
are centred with finite fourth moments, `s² = Var(∑ Xᵢ) > 0` and every neighbourhood has at most
`D` elements, then the law of `∑ Xᵢ / s` is within
`D² s⁻³ ∑ E|Xᵢ|³ + 4 D^(3/2) (√π s²)⁻¹ (∑ E Xᵢ⁴)^(1/2)` of the standard normal law.
Independence of each `Xᵢ` from the variables outside its neighbourhood alone does not give this.

For an exchangeable pair `(W, W′)` with `E[W′ | W] = (1 − a) W`, the distance of `W` from the
normal law is controlled by how far `E[(W′ − W)² | W]` is from `2a` and by `E|W′ − W|³`; for a
nonnegative `X` with a size-bias coupling `Xˢ`, by the variance of `E[Xˢ − X | X]` and by
`E(Xˢ − X)²`. On the Poisson side the roadmap proves the theorem of Arratia, Goldstein and Gordon
with its third term `b₃`, which needs no exact independence, and size-bias bounds: for a sum `W`
of indicators with success probabilities `pᵢ`, mean `λ` and an increasing size-bias coupling, the
law of `W` is within `2 (1 − e^(−λ))/λ · (Var W − λ + 2 ∑ pᵢ²)` of the Poisson law with mean `λ`.

The applications are explicit bounds for each number `n` of vertices, for triangles and isolated
vertices of `G(n, p)`, normal for `0 < p < 1` (from `n = 3`, resp. `n = 2`) and Poisson for every
`n` and `p`, and for head runs. For instance the number of triangles is within
`2 (1 − e^(−λ)) (p³ + 3 (n − 3) p² (1 − p))` of the Poisson law with mean `λ = C(n, 3) p³`.

The roadmap builds on SteinsMethodForNormalApproximation (the bound through the Stein class and
the neighbourhood bound in terms of moments), on PoissonApproximation (the Stein equation of the
Poisson law, its bounds and the local dependence theorem with exact independence), on
RandomGraphs (the triangle and isolated-vertex counts, and the Poisson limit of sparse
triangles, quantified here) and on Mathlib's binomial random graph.

Layers 1 and 2 prove the normal bounds for dependency graphs and for couplings, and Layer 3
applies them to `G(n, p)`. Layer 4 proves the Poisson bounds, and Layer 5 applies them
to `G(n, p)` and to head runs.

## Conventions

- **Random variables.** Variables live on a probability space `(Ω, P)`; the law of `W` is
  `P.map W`; `Var` is Mathlib's `ProbabilityTheory.variance` and `E[· | W]` is Mathlib's
  conditional expectation `P[· | MeasurableSpace.comap W inferInstance]` on the σ-algebra
  generated by `W`. Moment hypotheses are `MemLp` or `Integrable` hypotheses: the integral of a
  non-integrable function, and the variance of a function outside `L²`, are `0` in Lean, and the
  conditional expectation of a non-integrable function is `0`; every statement carries the
  hypotheses that exclude these values. Standard deviations are written `s` (with `unitInterval`
  open, `σ` is notation), rates `λ` or `lam`.
- **Normal side.** The standard normal law is Mathlib's `ProbabilityTheory.gaussianReal 0 1`, and
  `W₁(μ, ν)` is Tau Ceti's `TauCeti.wassersteinEDist 1 μ ν`, the distance of
  SteinsMethodForNormalApproximation, with values in `[0, ∞]`; bounds are written
  `W₁ ≤ ENNReal.ofReal (…)`. The Stein class of SteinsMethodForNormalApproximation 3.1 consists
  of differentiable `f` with `|f| ≤ 1`, `|f′| ≤ √(2/π)` and `f′` `2`-Lipschitz; for such `f`,
  `|f(y) − f(x) − (y − x) f′(x)| ≤ (y − x)²`, and with `F(w) = ∫₀ʷ f`,
  `|F(y) − F(x) − (y − x) f(x) − (y − x)² f′(x)/2| ≤ |y − x|³/3`.
- **Poisson side.** `Po(λ)` is Mathlib's `ProbabilityTheory.poissonMeasure λ`, and the distance
  is Mathlib's `MeasureTheory.tvDist`, which on probability laws on `ℕ` is `∑ₖ |μ{k} − ν{k}|`,
  with values in `[0, 2]`: twice the total variation distance of Ross's survey and equal to the
  norm of Arratia, Goldstein and Gordon. Every Poisson bound below carries this factor `2`. The
  factor `(1 − e^(−λ))/λ` is `0` at `λ = 0` (Lean's division), and `λ^(−1/2)` is `0` at `λ = 0`
  (Mathlib's `Real.zero_rpow`); every statement is true at `λ = 0` as written, where both sides
  are `0`.
- **Families of indicators** are given by their joint law, a probability measure `ξ` on
  `ι → Bool` for a finite type `ι`, as in PoissonApproximation: `occurrences x` is the number of
  `true` coordinates, `pᵢ = ξ{xᵢ = true}`, and `intensity ξ = ∑ pᵢ` is the mean `λ` of the
  count. Couplings are hypotheses: an exchangeable pair is `P.map (W, W′) = P.map (W′, W)`; a
  size-bias coupling is the identity `E[X f(X)] = μ E[f(Xˢ)]` for bounded measurable `f`; a
  coupling of two laws is Tau Ceti's `TauCeti.IsCoupling`. No size-bias law is defined here:
  Tau Ceti's `MeasureTheory.Measure.weighted` with weight `x` is the size-biased law of
  StandardDistributions 7.12, and `Xˢ` has it exactly when the identity holds.
- **Random graphs.** `G(n, p)` is Mathlib's `SimpleGraph.binomialRandom (Fin n) p` with
  `p : unitInterval`, the law under which the edges are independent with probability `p`. On a
  finite vertex type every set of graphs is measurable: `{G}` is the preimage of the singleton
  `{G.Adj}` under the measurable injective map `SimpleGraph.Adj`, and a finite set is a finite
  union of singletons. So every count of a graph has a genuine law. The edge indicators of
  `G(n, p)` are independent: `SimpleGraph.binomialRandom_apply` writes `G(n, p)` through the
  product `Measure.infinitePi` of Bernoulli laws on `Sym2 (Fin n)`, whose coordinates are
  independent by `ProbabilityTheory.iIndepFun_infinitePi`. The triangle count `triangleCount` and
  the isolated-vertex count `isolatedCount` are copied verbatim from RandomGraphs 11.2 and 11.3.
- **Coin tossing.** `n` independent tosses with head probability `p` are the law
  `Measure.pi (fun _ : Fin n => bernoulliMeasure true false p)` on `Fin n → Bool`, with `true`
  for heads.

## Layer 1: dependency graphs and the normal approximation in `W₁`

### 1.1 Dependency graphs and the variable model

Define `IsDependencyGraph P X N` for a finite family `X : ι → Ω → ℝ` and `N : ι → Finset ι`:
every `i ∈ Nᵢ`; `j ∈ Nᵢ` exactly when `i ∈ Nⱼ`; and for any two finite sets `A, B` of indices
such that no `b ∈ B` lies in `N_a` for any `a ∈ A`, the families `(X_a)_{a ∈ A}` and
`(X_b)_{b ∈ B}` are independent (Chatterjee, survey, p. 5, where the neighbourhoods are those of
an undirected graph and contain the vertex itself; there the method of dependency graphs is
traced to L. H. Y. Chen and its wider use to Baldi and Rinott). The
condition `i ∈ Nᵢ` makes the two sets in the third clause disjoint.

Prove `isDependencyGraph_of_measurable` (the variable model): let `(Y_v)_{v ∈ V}` be independent
random variables with values in measurable spaces `β_v`, let `vbl : ι → Finset V`, and let each
`Xᵢ` be measurable for the σ-algebra generated by the `Y_v` with `v ∈ vbl i`. Then
`Nᵢ = {i} ∪ {j | vbl i ∩ vbl j ≠ ∅}` is a dependency graph. (Proved here.)

Route. The first two clauses hold by construction. For the third, if no `b ∈ B` lies in any
`N_a`, `a ∈ A`, then `S = ⋃_{a ∈ A} vbl a` and `T = ⋃_{b ∈ B} vbl b` are disjoint. The σ-algebras
generated by `(Y_v)_{v ∈ S}` and `(Y_v)_{v ∈ T}` are independent by Mathlib's
`ProbabilityTheory.indep_iSup_of_disjoint`. The vector `(X_a)_{a ∈ A}` is measurable for the
first, since each coordinate is measurable for a smaller σ-algebra and the product σ-algebra on
`A → ℝ` is generated by the coordinates; likewise `(X_b)_{b ∈ B}` for the second. Independence
of the generating σ-algebras passes to the two vectors.

**Checks.**
- Pairwise independence is not enough. For independent signs `ε₁, ε₂, ε₃`, the variables
  `ε₁ε₂, ε₂ε₃, ε₁ε₃` are pairwise independent, but their product is `1`, so `Nᵢ = {i}` is not a
  dependency graph. The variable model gives the complete graph, since any two of the index sets
  `{1, 2}, {2, 3}, {1, 3}` meet.
- Independence of each variable from the variables outside its neighbourhood is not enough
  either. Let `ζ, ε₁, ε₂` be independent signs and `(X₁, X₂, X₃, X₄) = (ε₁, ε₁ζ, ε₂, ε₂ζ)` with
  `N₁ = N₂ = {1, 2}`, `N₃ = N₄ = {3, 4}`. Each `Xᵢ` is independent of the two variables outside
  its neighbourhood, but `X₁X₂X₃X₄ = ζ² = 1` while `E[X₁X₂] = E[X₃X₄] = 0`, so `(X₁, X₂)` and
  `(X₃, X₄)` are not independent.
- An index with `vbl i = ∅` has `Xᵢ` almost surely constant and `Nᵢ = {i}`.

*Needs:* Mathlib `ProbabilityTheory.iIndepFun`, `ProbabilityTheory.iIndepFun_iff_iIndep`,
`ProbabilityTheory.indep_iSup_of_disjoint`,
`ProbabilityTheory.IndepFun`, `MeasurableSpace.comap`.

### 1.2 The bound in terms of the largest neighbourhood

Prove `wassersteinEDist_le_of_isDependencyGraph`: let `X` have a dependency graph `N` with
`|Nᵢ| ≤ D` for every `i`, let every `Xᵢ` have a finite fourth moment and mean `0`, and let
`s² = Var(∑ Xᵢ)` with `s > 0`. Then

    W₁(law of s⁻¹ ∑ Xᵢ, N(0, 1)) ≤ D² s⁻³ ∑ᵢ E|Xᵢ|³ + 4 D^(3/2) (√π s²)⁻¹ √(∑ᵢ E Xᵢ⁴).

This is the bound of Ross's survey, Theorem 3.5, p. 12, with the constant `4` in place of `√26`.
There it is stated for neighbourhoods as in its Definition 3.1, where each `Xᵢ` is independent of
the variables outside `Nᵢ`; its proof (pp. 14–15) uses the independence of pairs of subfamilies,
and the statement is read here with the hypothesis of 1.1. (Proved here, with the count below.)

Route. Write `T = ∑ᵢ Xᵢ ∑_{j ∈ Nᵢ} Xⱼ`, `cᵢ = E|Xᵢ|³` and `aᵢ = E Xᵢ⁴`.

*Step 1: the bound in terms of `T`.* Taking `A = {i}` and `B = {j | j ∉ Nᵢ}` in the third clause
of 1.1 shows that `Xᵢ` is independent of `(Xⱼ)_{j ∉ Nᵢ}` (reindex `B` as the subtype
`{j // j ∉ Nᵢ}` and compose with the evaluation at `i`; measurable maps preserve independence).
So SteinsMethodForNormalApproximation 4.2 applies and gives

    W₁ ≤ s⁻³ ∑ᵢ E[|Xᵢ| (∑_{j ∈ Nᵢ} Xⱼ)²] + √(2/π) s⁻² √(Var T).

It remains to show `∑ᵢ E[|Xᵢ| (∑_{j ∈ Nᵢ} Xⱼ)²] ≤ D² ∑ cᵢ` and `Var T ≤ 8 D³ ∑ aᵢ`; then
`√(2/π) · √(8 D³ ∑ aᵢ) = 4 D^(3/2) √(∑ aᵢ) / √π`. All the expectations below are finite because
every `Xᵢ` is in `L⁴`, so every product of at most four of them is integrable (Hölder).

*Step 2: the first term.* Pointwise, `|Xᵢ| (∑_{j ∈ Nᵢ} Xⱼ)² ≤ ∑_{j ∈ Nᵢ} ∑_{k ∈ Nᵢ} |Xᵢ Xⱼ Xₖ|`
and, by the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means,
`|Xᵢ Xⱼ Xₖ| ≤ (|Xᵢ|³ + |Xⱼ|³ + |Xₖ|³)/3`, also when some of `i, j, k` coincide. Taking expectations
and summing over `i`, the right side is `(Σ₁ + Σ₂ + Σ₃)/3` with

    Σ₁ = ∑ᵢ ∑_{j,k ∈ Nᵢ} cᵢ = ∑ᵢ |Nᵢ|² cᵢ ≤ D² ∑ᵢ cᵢ,
    Σ₂ = ∑ᵢ ∑_{j,k ∈ Nᵢ} cⱼ = ∑ᵢ |Nᵢ| ∑_{j ∈ Nᵢ} cⱼ ≤ D ∑ⱼ cⱼ · #{i | j ∈ Nᵢ},

and `Σ₃ = Σ₂` by the symmetry `j ↔ k`. By the symmetry of the neighbourhoods,
`#{i | j ∈ Nᵢ} = #{i | i ∈ Nⱼ} = |Nⱼ| ≤ D`, so `Σ₂ ≤ D² ∑ cⱼ` and the sum is at most `D² ∑ cᵢ`.

*Step 3: the variance of `T`.* Let `Q` be the set of quadruples `(i, j, k, l)` with `j ∈ Nᵢ` and
`l ∈ Nₖ`; indices may coincide. Then `T = ∑_{(i,j)} XᵢXⱼ` over the pairs with `j ∈ Nᵢ`, and

    Var T = ∑_{(i,j,k,l) ∈ Q} Cov(XᵢXⱼ, XₖXₗ).

Write `M_{ij} = Nᵢ ∪ Nⱼ` and split `Q` into two cases.

- *Case (a): neither `k` nor `l` lies in `M_{ij}`.* Then no element of `B = {k, l}` lies in the
  neighbourhood of an element of `A = {i, j}`, so `(Xᵢ, Xⱼ)` and `(Xₖ, Xₗ)` are independent by
  1.1 (also when `i = j` or `k = l`, when `A` or `B` has one element), the products `XᵢXⱼ` and
  `XₖXₗ` are independent, and the covariance is `0`.
- *Case (b): `k ∈ M_{ij}` or `l ∈ M_{ij}`.* Let `S ⊆ Q` be the set of these quadruples. For each,
  `|Cov(XᵢXⱼ, XₖXₗ)| ≤ E|XᵢXⱼXₖXₗ| + E|XᵢXⱼ| E|XₖXₗ|`. Pointwise,
  `|XᵢXⱼXₖXₗ| ≤ (Xᵢ⁴ + Xⱼ⁴ + Xₖ⁴ + Xₗ⁴)/4`. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality twice,
  `E|XᵢXⱼ| ≤ (E Xᵢ² E Xⱼ²)^(1/2) ≤ (aᵢ aⱼ)^(1/4)`, likewise for `k, l`, and the inequality of
  arithmetic and geometric means gives `(aᵢ aⱼ aₖ aₗ)^(1/4) ≤ (aᵢ + aⱼ + aₖ + aₗ)/4`. These hold
  also when indices coincide. So `|Cov(XᵢXⱼ, XₖXₗ)| ≤ (aᵢ + aⱼ + aₖ + aₗ)/2` on `S`.

Hence `Var T ≤ ½ ∑_{S} (aᵢ + aⱼ + aₖ + aₗ)`. The set `S` is invariant under the three swaps
`(i, j, k, l) ↦ (j, i, k, l)`, `(i, j, k, l) ↦ (i, j, l, k)` and `(i, j, k, l) ↦ (k, l, i, j)`:

- the first swap keeps `Q` because `j ∈ Nᵢ ⇔ i ∈ Nⱼ`, and keeps the condition because
  `M_{ji} = M_{ij}`;
- the second keeps `Q` because `l ∈ Nₖ ⇔ k ∈ Nₗ`, and the condition is symmetric in `k, l`;
- for the third, the condition says that some `b ∈ {k, l}` lies in `N_a` for some
  `a ∈ {i, j}`, which by symmetry is the same as some `a ∈ {i, j}` lying in `N_b` for some
  `b ∈ {k, l}`, the condition for `(k, l, i, j)`.

The swaps carry `aⱼ`, `aₗ` and `aₖ` to `aᵢ`, `aₖ` and `aᵢ` respectively, so
`∑_{S} aⱼ = ∑_{S} aₖ = ∑_{S} aₗ = ∑_{S} aᵢ` and `Var T ≤ 2 ∑_{S} aᵢ`. Count: for fixed `i` and
`j ∈ Nᵢ`, the pairs `(k, l)` with `(i, j, k, l) ∈ S` are those with `k ∈ M_{ij}` and `l ∈ Nₖ`,
at most `|M_{ij}| D` of them, together with those with `l ∈ M_{ij}` and `l ∈ Nₖ`, that is
`k ∈ N_l`, at most `|M_{ij}| D` of them. As `|M_{ij}| ≤ |Nᵢ| + |Nⱼ| ≤ 2D`, there are at most
`4D²` such pairs, and at most `D` choices of `j`. So `∑_{S} aᵢ ≤ ∑ᵢ aᵢ · D · 4D² = 4D³ ∑ aᵢ`, and
`Var T ≤ 8 D³ ∑ aᵢ`.

**Checks.**
- Independence from the variables outside each neighbourhood does not suffice. Let
  `ζ, ε₁, …, ε_m` be independent signs, `X_{2a−1} = ε_a`, `X_{2a} = ε_a ζ` and
  `N_{2a−1} = N_{2a} = {2a − 1, 2a}`. Each `Xᵢ` is independent of the variables outside `Nᵢ`, the
  neighbourhoods are symmetric and contain `i`, `D = 2` and `s² = 2m`. The right side of 1.2 is
  `c/√(2m)` with `c = 4 + 8√2/√π = 10.383`. But `∑ Xᵢ = (1 + ζ) ∑ ε_a` vanishes when `ζ = −1`,
  so `E|∑ Xᵢ/s| ≤ 1/√2`, and since `x ↦ |x|` is `1`-Lipschitz,
  `W₁ ≥ E|Z| − 1/√2 = √(2/π) − 1/√2 = 0.0908` for every `m`. For `2m ≥ 13084` the right side is
  smaller. (The Check of 1.1 shows that the strong hypothesis fails here.)
- Independent summands with `Nᵢ = {i}`: `D = 1` and the bound is
  `s⁻³ ∑ E|Xᵢ|³ + 4 (√π s²)⁻¹ √(∑ E Xᵢ⁴)`. It also follows from SteinsMethodForNormalApproximation
  4.2 directly, where then `T = ∑ Xᵢ²` and `Var T = ∑ Var(Xᵢ²) ≤ ∑ E Xᵢ⁴`.
- One summand: `E|X|³ ≥ s³` and `E X⁴ ≥ s⁴`, so the right side is at least `1 + 4/√π = 3.26`,
  while `W₁ ≤ E|W| + E|Z| ≤ 1 + √(2/π)`.
- The count of Step 3, exactly: moving sums `Xᵢ = εᵢ + εᵢ₊₁` of five signs, four summands,
  `D = 3`: `Var T = 116 ≤ 8 · 27 · 32 = 6912`. A star, `X₁ = ε₁ + ε₂ + ε₃` and `Xᵢ₊₁ = εᵢ`,
  `D = 4`: `Var T = 108 ≤ 12288`. Four copies of one centred Bernoulli variable `B − 1/3` with the
  complete graph, `D = 4`: `Var T = 512/81 ≤ 4096/27`. Three pairs `(B_a, 2B_a)` of a Bernoulli
  variable with mean `1/10`, centred, `D = 2`: `Var T = 8748/625 ≤ 134028/625`. In each case also
  `E T = s²`.

*Needs:* 1.1 (the definition); SteinsMethodForNormalApproximation 4.2
(`wassersteinEDist_map_sum_le_of_dependencyNeighbourhoods`); Mathlib `ProbabilityTheory.IndepFun`,
`ProbabilityTheory.IndepFun.comp`, `ProbabilityTheory.IndepFun.integral_mul_eq_mul_integral`,
`ProbabilityTheory.variance`, `MeasureTheory.MemLp`.

## Layer 2: couplings for the normal law in `W₁`

### 2.1 Exchangeable pairs

Prove `wassersteinEDist_le_of_steinPair`: let `W, W′` be measurable, `(W, W′)` exchangeable, `W`
integrable, `E|W′ − W|³ < ∞`, and `E[W′ | W] = (1 − a) W` almost surely for some `a > 0`. Then

    W₁(law of W, N(0, 1)) ≤ √(2/π) E|1 − E[(W′ − W)² | W] / (2a)| + E|W′ − W|³ / (3a).

This is the inequality (3.18) in the proof of Theorem 3.7 of Ross's survey, pp. 17–18 (Stein's
method of exchangeable pairs, which Chatterjee's survey, p. 3, traces to Stein's 1972 paper);
there `0 < a ≤ 1` and `E W² = 1`,
neither of which the route uses. If moreover `E W² = 1` and `E(W′ − W)⁴ < ∞`, then
`E[(W′ − W)²] = 2a` (Ross, Proposition 3.6, p. 16: `E[W W′] = E[W E[W′ | W]] = (1 − a)`), so the
Cauchy–Schwarz inequality bounds the first term by `√Var(E[(W′ − W)² | W]) / (√(2π) a)`, which
is the form of Ross's Theorem 3.7. (Proved here.)

Route. Let `f` be in the Stein class and `F(w) = ∫₀ʷ f`. The function `G(x, y) = F(y) − F(x)` is
antisymmetric and `|G(x, y)| ≤ |y − x|`, so `G(W, W′)` is integrable and exchangeability gives
`E G(W, W′) = E G(W′, W) = −E G(W, W′)`, that is `E[F(W′) − F(W)] = 0`. By the second Taylor
bound of the Conventions,
`F(W′) − F(W) = (W′ − W) f(W) + ½ (W′ − W)² f′(W) + R` with `|R| ≤ |W′ − W|³/3`. Since `f(W)` is
bounded and measurable for the σ-algebra of `W`, and `W, W′` are integrable, the pull-out property
and the tower property of conditional expectation give
`E[(W′ − W) f(W)] = E[f(W) (E[W′ | W] − W)] = −a E[W f(W)]` and
`E[(W′ − W)² f′(W)] = E[E[(W′ − W)² | W] f′(W)]`. Hence

    E f′(W) − E[W f(W)] = E[f′(W) (1 − E[(W′ − W)² | W]/(2a))] − E[R]/a,

whose absolute value is at most the right side, because `|f′| ≤ √(2/π)`. Conclude with
SteinsMethodForNormalApproximation 3.1.

**Checks.**
- Sums of signs: `W = n^(−1/2) ∑ εᵢ`, and `W′` replaces a uniformly chosen `ε_I` by an independent
  copy. Then `a = 1/n`, `E[(W′ − W)² | W] = 2/n = 2a`, so the first term is `0`, and
  `E|W′ − W|³ = 4 n^(−3/2)`; the bound is `4/(3√n)`. For `n = 1` it is `4/3`, against the distance
  `0.5354` of a sign from the normal law.
- The constant `√(2/π)` of the first term cannot be lowered: for `W = W′ = 0` (any `a > 0`) the
  right side is `√(2/π)`, and `W₁(δ₀, N(0, 1)) = E|Z| = √(2/π)`.
- Values `a > 1` occur: for a symmetric sign `W` and `W′ = −W`, the pair is exchangeable with
  `a = 2`, `E[(W′ − W)² | W] = 4 = 2a`, and the bound is `8/6 = 4/3`.
- The integrability of `W` cannot be dropped: for `W′ = W` with no first moment and `a = 1`, the
  conditional expectation of the non-integrable `W′` is `0 = (1 − a) W`, exchangeability holds,
  `W′ − W = 0`, the right side is `√(2/π)` and `W₁` is infinite.

*Needs:* SteinsMethodForNormalApproximation 3.1 (`wassersteinEDist_le_iSup_steinClass` and its
`steinClass`); Mathlib `MeasureTheory.condExp`,
`MeasureTheory.condExp_mul_of_stronglyMeasurable_left`, `MeasureTheory.integral_condExp`.

### 2.2 Size-bias couplings

Prove `wassersteinEDist_le_of_sizeBias`: let `X ≥ 0` almost surely, with mean `μ` and variance
`s² > 0`, and let `Xˢ` be measurable with `E[X f(X)] = μ E[f(Xˢ)]` for every bounded measurable
`f`, and `E(Xˢ − X)² < ∞`. Then

    W₁(law of (X − μ)/s, N(0, 1)) ≤ μ s⁻² √(2/π) √(Var E[Xˢ − X | X]) + μ s⁻³ E(Xˢ − X)².

(Ross's survey, Theorem 3.13, p. 23, with Definition 3.7, p. 23; the method is that of Goldstein
and Rinott, 1996.)

Route. `X ∈ L²`, since its variance is the positive `s²`, and `μ > 0`, since `μ = 0` and `X ≥ 0`
would give `X = 0` almost surely. Taking `f = 1_{(−∞, 0)}` gives `μ P(Xˢ < 0) = 0`, so `Xˢ ≥ 0`
almost surely. With `f_R(x) = min(max(x, 0), R)` and monotone convergence, `E X² = μ E Xˢ`; so
`Xˢ` is integrable and `E[Xˢ − X] = s²/μ`. Let `f` be in the Stein class, `W = (X − μ)/s` and
`g(x) = f((x − μ)/s)`, a bounded measurable function. The coupling identity for `g` gives
`E[W f(W)] = s⁻¹ (E[X g(X)] − μ E g(X)) = (μ/s) E[g(Xˢ) − g(X)]`, and the first Taylor bound of
the Conventions gives `g(Xˢ) − g(X) = s⁻¹ (Xˢ − X) f′(W) + R` with `|R| ≤ s⁻² (Xˢ − X)²`. As
`f′(W)` is bounded and measurable for the σ-algebra of `X`,

    E f′(W) − E[W f(W)] = E[f′(W) (1 − μ s⁻² E[Xˢ − X | X])] − (μ/s) E[R].

The factor `1 − μ s⁻² E[Xˢ − X | X]` has mean `0`, so by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality its mean
absolute value is at most `μ s⁻² √(Var E[Xˢ − X | X])`; with `|f′| ≤ √(2/π)` and the bound on
`R`, conclude with SteinsMethodForNormalApproximation 3.1. (Proved here, following Ross.)

**Checks.**
- Binomial laws: `X = ∑ Xᵢ` for independent Bernoulli variables with mean `p ∈ (0, 1)`, and
  `Xˢ = X − X_I + 1` with `I` uniform and independent (Ross, Corollary 3.15, p. 25). Then
  `Var E[Xˢ − X | X] = p(1 − p)/n`, `E(Xˢ − X)² = 1 − p`, and the bound is
  `√(2/π) √(p/(n(1 − p))) + (n p (1 − p))^(−1/2)`; for `n = 4`, `p = 1/2` it is `1.399`, against
  the distance `0.2571`.
- Poisson laws: `Xˢ = X + 1` has the size-biased law at positive rate (Tau Ceti's
  `ProbabilityTheory.HasLaw.succ_poissonMeasure`); the variance term is `0` and the bound is
  `μ/s³ = λ^(−1/2)`.
- The sign condition cannot be dropped: for `X = −Y` with `Y` binomial with `n = 4`, `p = 1/2`, and
  `Xˢ = −Yˢ`, the identity holds with `μ = −2`, while the right side is
  `−2 · √(2/π) · (1/4) − 2 · (1/2) = −1.399 < 0`.

## Layer 3: normal approximation of counts in `G(n, p)`

### 3.1 Triangles

Prove `wassersteinEDist_triangleCount_le`: for `n ≥ 3` and `0 < p < 1`, with `q = p³`, `T` the
number of triangles of `G(n, p)` and

    s² = C(n, 3) q (1 − q + 3 (n − 3) p² (1 − p)),

which is `Var T`, the law of `(T − C(n, 3) q)/s` is within

    (3n − 8)² s⁻³ C(n, 3) q (1 − q) ((1 − q)² + q²)
      + 4 (3n − 8)^(3/2) (√π s²)⁻¹ √(C(n, 3) q (1 − q) ((1 − q)³ + q³))

of `N(0, 1)` in `W₁` (Ross's survey, §3.2.1, pp. 15–16, which applies its Theorem 3.5, with the
constant `√26` in place of `4`; the display there writes the Kolmogorov distance `d_K` on the
left, but what Theorem 3.5 bounds is `W₁`). For fixed `p` the bound is of order `1/n`; for
`p = n^(−α)` it tends to `0` when `α < 2/9`.

Route. For a three-element set `α ⊆ Fin n` let `Y_α` be the indicator that its three edges are
present. Then `triangleCount G = ∑_α Y_α(G)`, since `cliqueFinset 3` consists exactly of these
sets. Each `Y_α` is measurable for its three edge indicators, which are independent (Conventions),
so 1.1 gives a dependency graph `N_α = {β | α and β share an edge} = {β | |α ∩ β| ≥ 2}` with
`|N_α| = 1 + 3(n − 3) = 3n − 8`. The centred `X_α = Y_α − q` take the value `1 − q` with
probability `q` and `−q` otherwise, so `E|X_α|³ = q(1 − q)((1 − q)² + q²)` and
`E X_α⁴ = q(1 − q)((1 − q)³ + q³)`. For the variance, `Cov(Y_α, Y_β)` is `q(1 − q)` for `β = α`,
`p⁵ − p⁶` when `α` and `β` share one edge (there are `3(n − 3)` such `β`), and `0` otherwise; so
`Var T = C(n, 3) (q(1 − q) + 3(n − 3) p³ p²(1 − p))`, the stated `s²`, which is positive. Apply 1.2
with `D = 3n − 8`.

**Checks.**
- For `n = 4` every two of the four triangles of `K₄` share an edge, so `D = 4 = 3 · 4 − 8`; for
  `n = 3`, `D = 1` and `T` is a single Bernoulli variable with mean `p³`.
- The variance: at `n = 4`, `p = 1/2`, `s² = 4 · (1/8) · (7/8 + 3/8) = 5/8`, the exact variance of
  the triangle count of `G(4, 1/2)`.
- The moments: `E|X|³ = q(1 − q)³ + (1 − q)q³`, which is the stated `q(1 − q)((1 − q)² + q²)`; a
  formula with `q³` in place of `q²` would give `3/32` instead of `1/8` at `q = 1/2`.

`ProbabilityTheory.iIndepFun_infinitePi`, `SimpleGraph.cliqueFinset`, `SimpleGraph.measurable_adj`.

### 3.2 Isolated vertices

Prove `wassersteinEDist_isolatedCount_le`: for `n ≥ 2` and `0 < p < 1`, with `r = 1 − p`, `X` the
number of isolated vertices of `G(n, p)`,

    μ = n r^(n−1),   s² = μ (1 + (n p − 1) r^(n−2)),
    e₁ = (n − 1) p r^(n−2),   e₂ = e₁ + (n − 1)(n − 2) p² r^(2n−5),
    V₁ = n e₁ (1 − e₁) + n (n − 1) (p r^(2n−4) + (n − 2)² p² r^(2n−5) − e₁²),

the law of `(X − μ)/s` is within `μ s⁻² √(2/π) (√2/n) √(V₁ + s²) + μ s⁻³ (e₂ + 2e₁ + 1)` of
`N(0, 1)` in `W₁`. Here `μ` and `s²` are the mean and variance of `X`, `V₁` is the variance of the
number of vertices of degree one, and `e₁`, `e₂` are the first two moments of the number of
neighbours of degree one of a fixed vertex. At `n = 2` the terms with the exponent `2n − 5` carry
the factor `n − 2 = 0`. (Ross's survey, Example 3.9 and Theorem 3.17, pp. 25–27, with the coupling
and the bounds (3.28)–(3.30); the explicit bound is assembled here.) If `n^α p → c ∈ (0, ∞)` with
`1 ≤ α < 2`, the bound is of order `1/s` (Ross, Theorem 3.17).

Route. On `Ω = SimpleGraph (Fin n) × Fin n` with the law `G(n, p) ⊗ uniform` (every set is
measurable, Conventions), let `X(G, I)` be the isolated-vertex count of `G` and `Xˢ(G, I)` that of
`G` with the edges at `I` erased. *The coupling.* Given that a vertex `v` is isolated, the other
edges of `G(n, p)` are still independent with probability `p`, so the indicators of the other
vertices have, given `v` isolated, the law they have after the edges at `v` are erased. Hence for
bounded `f`, `E[X f(X)] = ∑_v P(v isolated) E[f(X) | v isolated] = μ E f(Xˢ)`, all vertices having
the same probability `μ/n` of being isolated (Ross, Corollary 3.16, p. 25). *The difference.*
Erasing the edges at `I` makes `I` isolated, which adds `1` if its degree `d_I` was positive, and
isolates the neighbours of `I` of degree one, `D_I` of them; so `Xˢ − X = D_I + 1[d_I > 0]` and
`E[Xˢ − X | G] = n⁻¹ ∑ᵢ (Dᵢ + 1[dᵢ > 0])`. Since the σ-algebra of `X` lies in that of `G`, the tower
property and the `L²` contraction of conditional expectation give
`Var E[Xˢ − X | X] ≤ Var E[Xˢ − X | G]`. Now `∑ᵢ Dᵢ` is the number `Y` of vertices of degree one
and `∑ᵢ 1[dᵢ > 0] = n − X`, so `Var E[Xˢ − X | G] = n⁻² Var(Y − X) ≤ 2 n⁻² (V₁ + s²)`.
Also `E(Xˢ − X)² ≤ n⁻¹ ∑ᵢ E(Dᵢ + 1)² = E D₁² + 2 E D₁ + 1 = e₂ + 2e₁ + 1`. *The moments.* A vertex
is isolated with probability `r^(n−1)` and two vertices with probability `r^(2n−3)`, which gives
`μ` and `s² = μ(1 − r^(n−1)) + n(n − 1) p r^(2n−3)`, the stated form. A vertex has degree one with
probability `e₁`; two vertices both have degree one with probability `p r^(2n−4)` if they are
joined and `(1 − p) ((n − 2) p r^(n−3))²` if not, which gives
`V₁`. A fixed vertex has `D₁` neighbours
of degree one with `E D₁ = (n − 1) p r^(n−2) = e₁`, and two distinct neighbours `u, w` of degree
one need the edges to `u` and `w` and the absence of the `2n − 5` other edges at `u` or `w`, which
gives `E D₁² = e₂`. Apply 2.2.

**Checks.**
- At `n = 3`, `p = 1/2`: `μ = 3/4`, `s² = 15/16`, `e₁ = 1/2`, `e₂ = 3/4` and `V₁ = 3/4`, each the
  exact value of the corresponding moment of `G(3, 1/2)`. A formula with `(n − 1)²` in place of
  `(n − 2)²` in `V₁` would give `3`.
- The coupling at `n = 3`, `p = 1/2`: `P(Xˢ = k) = k P(X = k)/μ` for every `k`, as the size-bias
  identity requires.
- At `n = 2`: `X` is `2` when the edge is absent and `0` otherwise, `μ = 2(1 − p)`,
  `s² = 4p(1 − p)` and `V₁ = 4p(1 − p)`.

*Needs:* 2.2; RandomGraphs 11.3 (`isolatedCount`, copied); Mathlib `SimpleGraph.binomialRandom`,
`SimpleGraph.binomialRandom_apply`, `ProbabilityTheory.iIndepFun_infinitePi`,
`MeasureTheory.Measure.prod`, `ProbabilityTheory.uniformOn`, `MeasureTheory.condExp_condExp_of_le`,
`MeasureTheory.eLpNorm_condExp_le_eLpNorm`.

For `p = 1/2` and `n = 6`, the bound of 3.1 is `32.6` and that of 3.2 is `3.11`: the bounds are
informative only for larger `n`, at rate `1/n` in 3.1 for fixed `p` and at rate `1/s` in 3.2.

## Layer 4: Poisson approximation beyond exact local independence

### 4.1 The term `b₃`

Prove `tvDist_le_of_neighbourhoods`: let `ξ` be the law of a family of indicators and
`N : ι → Finset ι` with `i ∈ Nᵢ`, with no independence assumed. With `λ = intensity ξ`,

    b₁ = ∑ᵢ ∑_{j ∈ Nᵢ} pᵢ pⱼ,   b₂ = ∑ᵢ ∑_{j ∈ Nᵢ, j ≠ i} ξ{xᵢ = xⱼ = true},
    b₃ = ∑ᵢ E| E[1{xᵢ = true} | 𝓕ᵢ] − pᵢ |,   𝓕ᵢ = σ(xⱼ : j ∉ Nᵢ),

the law of `occurrences` under `ξ` is within
`2 ((1 − e^(−λ))/λ · (b₁ + b₂) + min(1, λ^(−1/2)) · b₃)` of `Po(λ)`. The σ-algebra `𝓕ᵢ` is
generated by the coordinates outside `Nᵢ`; in Lean it is
`MeasurableSpace.comap (fun x (j : {j // j ∉ N i}) => x j) inferInstance`, the inverse image of
the product σ-algebra on `{j // j ∉ N i} → Bool`, and the conditional expectation is taken under
`ξ`. (Arratia, Goldstein and Gordon, Theorem 1, p. 11, with `b₁, b₂, b₃` defined on p. 10; they
have `1 ∧ 1.4 λ^(−1/2)` in place of `min(1, λ^(−1/2))`, and the sharper factor comes from the
bound of PoissonApproximation 2.3. Their normalization is that of `tvDist`.)

Route. Let `λ > 0` (at `λ = 0` every `pᵢ = 0`, the count is `0` almost surely and both sides are
`0`). For `A ⊆ ℕ` let `f` be the solution `steinSolution λ A` of PoissonApproximation 2.2, so that
`ξ(W ∈ A) − Po(λ)(A) = E[λ f(W + 1) − W f(W)]` with `W = ∑ xᵢ`. Write `Wᵢ = W − xᵢ` and
`Vᵢ = ∑_{j ∉ Nᵢ} xⱼ`; then `W f(W) = ∑ᵢ xᵢ f(Wᵢ + 1)` and

    E[λ f(W + 1) − W f(W)] = ∑ᵢ pᵢ E[f(W + 1) − f(Wᵢ + 1)]
                             + ∑ᵢ E[(pᵢ − xᵢ)(f(Wᵢ + 1) − f(Vᵢ + 1))] + ∑ᵢ E[(pᵢ − xᵢ) f(Vᵢ + 1)].

By the bound `|Δf| ≤ (1 − e^(−λ))/λ` of PoissonApproximation 2.3 and telescoping, the first sum is
at most `(1 − e^(−λ))/λ · ∑ pᵢ²`, since `W − Wᵢ = xᵢ`, and the second at most
`(1 − e^(−λ))/λ · ∑ᵢ ∑_{j ∈ Nᵢ, j ≠ i} (pᵢ pⱼ + ξ{xᵢ = xⱼ = true})`, since
`Wᵢ − Vᵢ = ∑_{j ∈ Nᵢ, j ≠ i} xⱼ` and `|pᵢ − xᵢ| ≤ pᵢ + xᵢ`; together `(1 − e^(−λ))/λ · (b₁ + b₂)`,
using `i ∈ Nᵢ`. In the third sum `f(Vᵢ + 1)` is bounded and `𝓕ᵢ`-measurable, so the pull-out and
tower properties give `E[(pᵢ − xᵢ) f(Vᵢ + 1)] = E[(pᵢ − E[xᵢ | 𝓕ᵢ]) f(Vᵢ + 1)]`, at most
`min(1, λ^(−1/2)) E|E[xᵢ | 𝓕ᵢ] − pᵢ|` by the bound `|f| ≤ min(1, λ^(−1/2))` of
PoissonApproximation 2.3. Conclude with Tau Ceti's `MeasureTheory.etvDist_le_two_mul_iff`.

**Checks.**
- If each `xᵢ` is independent of `(xⱼ)_{j ∉ Nᵢ}`, then `E[xᵢ | 𝓕ᵢ] = pᵢ`, `b₃ = 0`, and the
  statement is PoissonApproximation 3.1.
- Two equal indicators with probability `1/2` and `Nᵢ = {i}`: `λ = 1`, `b₁ = 1/2`, `b₂ = 0`, and
  `E[x₁ | x₂] = x₂ = x₁`, so `b₃ = 2 · 1/2 = 1`. The bound is `2 + (1 − e^(−1)) = 2.632`, the
  distance `2 − 3e^(−1) = 0.8964`. With the unconditional mean `E xᵢ` in place of the conditional
  expectation, `b₃` would be `0` and the right side `1 − e^(−1) = 0.632` would be false.
- A Markov chain of three indicators with `P(x₁ = 1) = 1/10` and transition probabilities
  `P(xₜ₊₁ = 1 | xₜ = 1) = 1/5`, `P(xₜ₊₁ = 1 | xₜ = 0) = 1/10`, with `Nᵢ = {i − 1, i, i + 1}`:
  `b₃ = 9/2500`, the bound is `0.289` and the distance `0.0164`.

*Needs:* PoissonApproximation 2.2 (`steinSolution`, `steinSolution_stein`), 2.3
(`abs_steinSolution_succ_sub_le`, `abs_steinSolution_le`), 3.1 (`occurrences`, `intensity`,
copied); Mathlib `MeasureTheory.condExp`, `MeasureTheory.condExp_mul_of_stronglyMeasurable_left`,

`MeasureTheory.etvDist_le_two_mul_iff`.

### 4.2 Size-bias and increasing couplings

Prove `tvDist_le_of_sizeBias`: let `W, Wˢ : Ω → ℕ` be measurable, with mean `E W = λ ≥ 0`,
`E[W f(W)] = λ E[f(Wˢ)]` for every bounded `f : ℕ → ℝ`, and `W + 1 − Wˢ` integrable.
Then the law of `W` is within `2 (1 − e^(−λ)) E|W + 1 − Wˢ|` of `Po(λ)` (Ross's survey,
Theorem 4.10, p. 44, with `min(1, λ)` in place of `1 − e^(−λ)` in the half normalization).

Prove `tvDist_le_of_increasingCoupling`: let `ξ` be the law of a family of indicators, and
suppose that for each `i` with `pᵢ ≠ 0` there is a coupling `γ` of `ξ` with the conditioned law
`ξ[· | xᵢ = true]` under which, almost surely, `xⱼ ≤ yⱼ` for every `j ≠ i`, where `(x, y)` is the
pair of coordinates. Then, with `W` the count and `λ = intensity ξ`, the law of `W` is within
`2 (1 − e^(−λ))/λ · (Var W − λ + 2 ∑ pᵢ²)` of `Po(λ)` (Ross's survey, Theorem 4.14, p. 47, with
`min(1, 1/λ)` in the half normalization, after Barbour, Holst and Janson). Ross's proofs use
only the bound on increments of the Stein solution, so they give the factor of
PoissonApproximation 2.3, and `λ · (1 − e^(−λ))/λ = 1 − e^(−λ)` in the first statement.

Route for the first. `W` is integrable: for `λ > 0` because its integral is `λ ≠ 0`, and for
`λ = 0` because the identity with `f` the indicator of `{k}` gives `k P(W = k) = 0` for every
`k ≥ 1`, so that `W = 0` almost surely and both sides are `0`. Let `λ > 0`. For
`A ⊆ ℕ` and `f = steinSolution λ A`, PoissonApproximation 2.2 and the coupling identity give
`P(W ∈ A) − Po(λ)(A) = E[λ f(W + 1) − W f(W)] = λ E[f(W + 1) − f(Wˢ)]`, and telescoping over the
`|W + 1 − Wˢ|` unit steps between `Wˢ` and `W + 1` with the bound of PoissonApproximation 2.3
bounds it by `(1 − e^(−λ)) E|W + 1 − Wˢ|`. Conclude with `MeasureTheory.etvDist_le_two_mul_iff`.

Route for the second. Let `λ > 0`. For `f = steinSolution λ A`,
`E[W f(W)] = ∑ᵢ pᵢ E[f(W) | xᵢ]`, where under `ξ[· | xᵢ = true]` the count is
`∑_{j ≠ i} yⱼ + 1`; and `λ E f(W + 1) = ∑ᵢ pᵢ E f(W + 1)`.
Under the coupling `γᵢ` for index `i` (indices with `pᵢ = 0` contribute nothing),

    E[λ f(W + 1) − W f(W)] = ∑ᵢ pᵢ E_{γᵢ}[f(∑ⱼ xⱼ + 1) − f(∑_{j ≠ i} yⱼ + 1)],

which by PoissonApproximation 2.3 is at most `(1 − e^(−λ))/λ · ∑ᵢ pᵢ E_{γᵢ}|xᵢ − Sᵢ|` with
`Sᵢ = ∑_{j ≠ i} (yⱼ − xⱼ) ≥ 0` almost surely. So `|xᵢ − Sᵢ| ≤ xᵢ + Sᵢ`, and
`E_{γᵢ}[xᵢ + Sᵢ] = pᵢ + (E[W | xᵢ = true] − 1) − (λ − pᵢ)`. Since `∑ᵢ pᵢ E[W | xᵢ = true] = E W²`,
the sum is `∑ pᵢ (2pᵢ − 1 − λ) + E W² = Var W − λ + 2 ∑ pᵢ²`. Conclude as before.

**Checks.**
- Independent indicators satisfy the second hypothesis with `y` equal to `x` except `yᵢ = true`;
  then `Var W − λ + 2 ∑ pᵢ² = ∑ pᵢ²` and the bound is that of PoissonApproximation 3.2.
- The first bound is attained: for one Bernoulli variable `W` with mean `p`, `Wˢ = 1`,
  `E|W + 1 − Wˢ| = p`, and the bound `2p(1 − e^(−p))` is the exact distance (PoissonApproximation
  1.1); at `p = 1/2` both are `0.3935`.
- Two equal indicators with probability `1/2` satisfy the second hypothesis (given `x₁ = true`,
  `x₂ = true`), with `Var W − λ + 2 ∑ pᵢ² = 1 − 1 + 1 = 1`: the bound is `2(1 − e^(−1)) = 1.264`,
  the distance `0.8964`. They violate the decreasing hypothesis of PoissonApproximation 3.4.
- An exchangeable family, mixing independent indicators with probability `1/10` or `1/5` with
  weights `1/2` each, four indicators: conditioning on `xᵢ = true` raises the mixing weight of `1/5`
  to `2/3`, a monotone coupling exists, and the bound `0.180` exceeds the distance `0.0473`.

*Needs:* PoissonApproximation 2.2 (`steinSolution_stein`), 2.3 (`abs_steinSolution_succ_sub_le`),
3.1 (`occurrences`, `intensity`, copied); Mathlib `ProbabilityTheory.cond`,
`ProbabilityTheory.variance`, `MeasureTheory.tvDist`; Tau Ceti `TauCeti.IsCoupling`,
`MeasureTheory.etvDist_le_two_mul_iff`.

## Layer 5: Poisson approximation of counts

### 5.1 Triangles

Prove `tvDist_triangleCount_le`: for every `n` and `p`, with `λ = C(n, 3) p³`, the law of the
triangle count of `G(n, p)` is within `2 (1 − e^(−λ)) (p³ + 3 (n − 3) p² (1 − p))` of `Po(λ)`
(Ross's survey, Theorem 4.15, p. 48, for the triangle, with `min(1, 1/λ)` in the half
normalization and `Var W − λ + 2λ p³` evaluated). For `p = c/n` the bound is of order `1/n`; with
`n pₙ → c` it gives again the Poisson limit of RandomGraphs 11.2.

Route. Index the triangle indicators `Y_α` by the three-element sets `α`, so that the count is the
number of occurrences of the family. For each `α`, the graph `G ∪ α` obtained by adding the edges
of `α` has, by the independence of the edges, the law of `G(n, p)` conditioned on `α ⊆ G`, and
`Y_β(G) ≤ Y_β(G ∪ α)` for every `β`; so the law of `(Y(G), Y(G ∪ α))` is an increasing coupling
as in 4.2. By the variance of the route of 3.1, `Var W − λ + 2 ∑ p_α² = Var W − λ + 2λp³` equals
`λ (p³ + 3 (n − 3) p² (1 − p))`, and 4.2 gives the bound for `λ > 0`. If `λ = 0` (`n < 3` or
`p = 0`), the count is `0` almost surely and both sides are `0`.

**Checks.**
- `n = 3`: the count is a Bernoulli variable with mean `q = p³`, and the bound `2q(1 − e^(−q))` is
  the exact distance.
- `n = 4`, `p = 1`: the count is `4`, the distance is `2(1 − 32 e^(−4)/3) = 1.6093` and the bound
  `2(1 − e^(−4)) = 1.9634`.
- `n = 6`, `p = 1/10`: `λ = 1/50`, the distance is `0.00274` and the bound `0.00325`.

*Needs:* 4.2 (`tvDist_le_of_increasingCoupling`); the variance of 3.1's route; RandomGraphs 11.2
(`triangleCount`, copied); Mathlib `SimpleGraph.binomialRandom`,
`SimpleGraph.binomialRandom_apply`, `ProbabilityTheory.iIndepFun_infinitePi`.

### 5.2 Isolated vertices

Prove `tvDist_isolatedCount_le`: for every `n` and `p`, with `λ = n (1 − p)^(n−1)`, the law of
the number of isolated vertices of `G(n, p)` is within
`2 (1 − e^(−λ)) ((1 − p)^(n−1) + (n − 1) p (1 − p)^(n−2))` of `Po(λ)`, the exponents in natural
numbers (Ross's survey, Example 4.5 and Proposition 4.13, pp. 45–46, with `min(1, λ)` in the
half normalization). For `p = (log n + c)/n` the bound is of order `log n / n`.

Route. Let `n ≥ 1`, and let `Wˢ(G)` be the number of isolated vertices of `G` with the edges at
the vertex `0` erased. As in the route of 3.2, given that `0` is isolated the other indicators
have the law they have after erasing the edges at `0`, and all vertices are alike, so
`E[W f(W)] = n P(0 isolated) E f(Wˢ) = λ E f(Wˢ)` (Ross, Corollary 4.12, p. 45). Erasing edges
never destroys an isolated vertex, so `|W + 1 − Wˢ|` is the indicator that `0` is isolated plus
the number of vertices `j ≠ 0` that become isolated by the erasing, whose mean is
`(1 − p)^(n−1) + (n − 1) p (1 − p)^(n−2)`: a
vertex `j ≠ 0` becomes isolated exactly when its only edge is the one to `0`. Apply the first
bound of 4.2. For `n = 0` both sides are `0`.

**Checks.**
- `n = 1`: the count is `1`, `λ = 1`, and both sides are `2(1 − e^(−1)) = 1.2642`.
- `n = 2`, `p = 1/2`: the count is `0` or `2` with probability `1/2` each, `λ = 1`, the distance is
  `2 − 3e^(−1) = 0.8964` and the bound `2(1 − e^(−1)) = 1.2642`.
- `p = 1` and `n ≥ 2`: `λ = 0` and both sides are `0`.

*Needs:* 4.2 (`tvDist_le_of_sizeBias`); RandomGraphs 11.3 (`isolatedCount`, copied); Mathlib
`SimpleGraph.binomialRandom`, `SimpleGraph.binomialRandom_apply`,
`ProbabilityTheory.iIndepFun_infinitePi`.

### 5.3 Head runs

Define `headRunCount k y` for `y : Fin n → Bool`: the number of positions `i` with `i + k ≤ n`
such that `y` is `true` at `i, …, i + k − 1` and, if `i > 0`, `false` at `i − 1`; this is the
number of runs of heads of length at least `k`, each counted once at its start.

Prove `tvDist_headRunCount_le`: let `y` be `n` independent tosses with head probability `p`, let
`1 ≤ k ≤ n`, `W = headRunCount k y` and `λ = p^k ((n − k)(1 − p) + 1)`. Then the law of `W` is
within `2 (1 − e^(−λ)) (λ (2k + 1)/(n − k + 1) + 2 p^k)` of `Po(λ)`, and the probability that no
`k` consecutive tosses are heads is within `(1 − e^(−λ)) (λ (2k + 1)/(n − k + 1) + 2 p^k)` of
`e^(−λ)` (Ross's survey, Theorem 4.8, p. 42, in the half normalization and without the factor
`(1 − e^(−λ))/λ`; Arratia, Goldstein and Gordon, Example 3, p. 16, for runs in an infinite
sequence).

Route. Let `xᵢ` be the indicator of a run starting at `i`; `xᵢ = 0` when `i + k > n`. It is a
function of the tosses `i − 1, …, i + k − 1` that exist, so the argument of 1.1, with the
independence of the tosses (Mathlib's `ProbabilityTheory.iIndepFun_pi`), shows that `xᵢ` is
independent of `(xⱼ)_{|j − i| > k}`; take `Nᵢ = {j | |i − j| ≤ k}`. Two runs cannot start at
distance at most `k` from each other (the second would need a tail inside the first), so `b₂ = 0`
in PoissonApproximation 3.1, which gives `tvDist ≤ 2 (1 − e^(−λ))/λ · b₁`, with
`p₀ = p^k` and `pᵢ = (1 − p) p^k` for `0 < i ≤ n − k`, so `λ` is as stated. For the count, the
pairs with `i = 0` or `j = 0` contribute at most `2 p₀ λ = 2λ p^k`; the pairs with `i, j > 0`
number at most `(n − k)(2k + 1)` and contribute at most `(n − k)(2k + 1)(1 − p)² p^(2k)`, which
is at most `λ² (2k + 1)/(n − k + 1)` because `λ ≥ (n − k + 1)(1 − p) p^k`. Hence
`b₁ ≤ λ² (2k + 1)/(n − k + 1) + 2λ p^k`; this gives the first bound for `λ > 0`, and at `λ = 0`
(`p = 0`) both sides are `0`. The event that no `k` consecutive tosses are heads is `{W = 0}`, and
`tvDist` is at least twice the difference of the masses at `0`.

**Checks.**
- The declumping: for `HHHTHH` and `k = 2` the count is `2`; counting every position followed by
  `k` heads would give `3`.
- `p = 1`: the tosses are all heads, `W = 1`, `λ = 1`, the distance is `2(1 − e^(−1)) = 1.2642`,
  and the bound is at least twice that.
- `k = n`: `W` is the indicator that all tosses are heads, `λ = p^n`, and the bound is
  `2(1 − e^(−λ))(λ(2n + 1) + 2λ)`.

*Needs:* PoissonApproximation 3.1 (`tvDist_le_of_dependencyNeighbourhoods`); the argument of 1.1;
Mathlib `MeasureTheory.Measure.pi`, `ProbabilityTheory.bernoulliMeasure`,
`ProbabilityTheory.iIndepFun_pi`, `MeasureTheory.tvDist`.

### Examples

Isolated vertices near the connectivity threshold `p = (log n + c)/n`, where `λ → e^(−c)` and the
bound of 5.2 is of order `log n / n`; triangles at `p = c/n`; the longest head run, which is below
`k` exactly when `W = 0` in 5.3, near `k = log(n(1 − p))/log(1/p)`.

## Scope and ownership

This roadmap owns: dependency graphs in the sense of 1.1 and the variable model; the normal bound
in terms of the largest neighbourhood (1.2); the normal bounds for exchangeable pairs and
size-bias couplings (Layer 2) and their consequences for triangles and isolated vertices of
`G(n, p)` (Layer 3); the theorem of Arratia, Goldstein and Gordon with the term `b₃` (4.1); the
Poisson bounds for size-bias and increasing couplings (4.2); and the explicit Poisson bounds for
triangles, isolated vertices and head runs (Layer 5).

Stated by other roadmaps and used here:

- SteinsMethodForNormalApproximation owns the Stein equation of the normal law, the bounds on its
  solution, the Stein class and the bound of `W₁` through it (3.1), the bound for independent sums
  (4.1), and the bound under dependency neighbourhoods in terms of the moments of
  `T = ∑ᵢ Xᵢ ∑_{j ∈ Nᵢ} Xⱼ` (4.2). That bound needs only the independence of each `Xᵢ` from the
  variables outside `Nᵢ`; the bound of 1.2 in terms of the largest neighbourhood needs the
  stronger hypothesis of 1.1, and the Check of 1.2 shows that it fails without it.
- PoissonApproximation owns the Stein characterization of the Poisson law, the solution of its
  Stein equation and both bounds on it (Layer 2), the local dependence theorem with exact
  independence (3.1, the case `b₃ = 0` of 4.1), the bound of Barbour and Hall and Le Cam's
  inequality (3.2, 3.3), the theorem on decreasing couplings (3.4), and the birthday problem,
  fixed points of a uniform permutation and empty boxes (Layer 4). `occurrences` and `intensity`
  are copied verbatim from its 3.1.
- RandomGraphs owns the triangle and isolated-vertex counts (copied verbatim from its 11.2 and
  11.3), the factorial-moment criterion for Poisson limits (11.1) and the Poisson limit of the
  triangle count when `n pₙ → c` (11.2); 5.1 bounds the distance at every `n` and `p`.
- StandardDistributions 7.12 owns size biasing as a weighted law; here size bias is the coupling
  identity of 2.2 and 4.2.

Not treated here: normal approximation in the Kolmogorov or total variation distance, which needs
the concentration inequalities of Chen and Shao, and Berry–Esseen bounds for dependent sums;
zero-bias couplings; Poisson approximation by exchangeable pairs; counts of general subgraphs of
`G(n, p)` and the full range of the triangle normal approximation (`n p → ∞`); implicit monotone
couplings through Strassen's theorem; Poisson process and compound Poisson approximation; and the
Lovász local lemma, whose dependency digraphs of events are a different notion. The
Malliavin–Stein method and the fourth moment theorem, with Stein bounds in total variation and
Kolmogorov distance for the normal law, are not treated here.

A public Lean development, Asher Yan's ProbabilityApproximation (Apache-2.0), proves a nonuniform
Berry–Esseen bound by Stein's method; it has no dependency graphs, couplings or Poisson
approximation, and nothing here is taken from it.
