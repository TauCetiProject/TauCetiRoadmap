# Roadmap: the Sorgenfrey line, the Niemytzki plane and the closure–complement theorem

**Summary.** This roadmap builds three classical objects of general topology and proves the
properties that make them standard counterexamples. The Sorgenfrey line is the real line with
the topology generated by the half-open intervals `[a, b)`; the Niemytzki (tangent-disc) plane is
the closed upper half-plane in which a point of the axis has as neighbourhoods the open discs
tangent to the axis at that point, together with the point itself; and the closure–complement
orbit of a set is the family of all sets obtained from it by repeated closure and complement.

The headline theorems are these.

- The Sorgenfrey line is hereditarily Lindelöf, perfectly normal and paracompact, and every
  compact subset of it is countable; its square is neither Lindelöf nor paracompact. So the
  Lindelöf property and paracompactness are not preserved by finite products.
- The Niemytzki plane is completely regular (T₃½), separable and not normal.
- For every closure operator on a Boolean algebra (monotone, extensive and idempotent, with no
  additivity assumed) the closure–complement orbit of an element has at most 14 elements, and the
  closure–interior orbit at most 7, where the interior is the complement of the closure of the
  complement. In an extremally disconnected space every closure–complement orbit has at most 10
  elements.

The roadmap builds on Mathlib's general topology: its separation, Lindelöf and paracompactness
classes, its closure operators, and its form of Jones' lemma (a separable normal space has no
closed discrete subset of the size of the continuum), which shows that the Sorgenfrey square and
the Niemytzki plane are not normal.
The three layers are independent. Layer 1 builds the Sorgenfrey line and its square. Layer 2
builds the Niemytzki plane. Layer 3 treats closure, interior and complement, first for an
abstract closure operator and then for topological closure in an extremally disconnected space.

## Conventions

- `Sorgenfrey` is a new type equal to `ℝ` by definition, with the topology generated by the sets
  `Ico a b` for real `a`, `b` (empty when `b ≤ a`). It carries no order or algebraic instances; a
  route that uses the order of `ℝ` reads a point of `Sorgenfrey` as a real number. The plane is
  `Sorgenfrey × Sorgenfrey` with the product topology.
- `NiemytzkiPlane` is a new type equal to `{p : ℝ × ℝ // 0 ≤ p.2}` by definition. Write `E` for
  the points with `0 < p.2` (the open half-plane) and `L` for the points with `p.2 = 0` (the
  axis). Its topology is generated by two families: the sets of points `q` of `E` with
  `(q.1 − c.1)² + (q.2 − c.2)² < r²` for any centre `c` and radius `r`, and, for `t : ℝ` and
  `r > 0`, the set `{(t, 0)} ∪ D(t, r)`, where `D(t, r)` is the open disc of radius `r` centred
  at `(t, r)`. A point `q` with `q.2 > 0` lies in `D(t, r)` iff `|q − (t, 0)|² < 2 r q.2`.
  Every point of `D(t, r)` has positive second coordinate.
- Mathlib's names are used for the separation classes: `T6Space` is T₀ and perfectly normal (normal
  with every closed set a Gδ); `T35Space` is T₀ and completely regular, where completely regular
  means that a point and a closed set not containing it are separated by a continuous map into
  `[0, 1]`; `ParacompactSpace` asks for a locally finite open refinement of every open cover.
- For a closure operator `c` on a Boolean algebra the interior is `i x = (c xᶜ)ᶜ`. For a
  topological space and `c = closure` this is the topological interior (Mathlib's
  `interior_compl`). The closure–complement orbit of `a` is the set of `b` with
  `Relation.ReflTransGen (fun x y => y = c x ∨ y = xᶜ) a b`; the closure–interior orbit uses the
  moves `y = c x` and `y = (c xᶜ)ᶜ`. Sizes are counted with `Set.encard`, which is infinite on an
  infinite set, so a bound by a natural number also says the orbit is finite.
- Words in `k` (closure) and `i` (interior) act on the left: `kis` is `k (i s)`.

## Layer 1: the Sorgenfrey line and the Sorgenfrey plane

### 1.1 Definitions

Define `Sorgenfrey` and its topology as in the Conventions. Two facts are used throughout.
Each `[a, b)` is clopen: its complement is the union of the open sets `[a − n, a)` and
`[b, b + n)` over `n : ℕ`. A set is open iff for each of its points `x` it contains some
`[x, y)` with `x < y`, so every Euclidean open set is open (the topology is finer than that of
`ℝ`) and the half-lines `(−∞, x)` and `[x, ∞)` are clopen.

**Checks.**

- The half-open intervals cannot be replaced by open intervals: those generate the usual
  topology of `ℝ`, in which `[0, 1]` is compact and uncountable (so 1.5 fails) and the square
  `ℝ²` is metrizable and second countable, hence paracompact and Lindelöf (so 1.6 fails).

### 1.2 The Sorgenfrey line is hereditarily Lindelöf

Prove `hereditarilyLindelofSpace_sorgenfrey`: every subspace of `Sorgenfrey` is Lindelöf.
(Proved here. The Lindelöf property of the whole line is [Ga30], Example 3.)

Route. *A countability lemma on `ℝ`.* Let `V` and `W` be sets of reals such that every
`x ∈ V \ W` has some `b(x) > x` with `(x, b(x)) ⊆ W`. Then `V \ W` is countable: for `x < y` in
`V \ W`, the point `y` is not in `(x, b(x)) ⊆ W`, so `y ≥ b(x)`; hence the nonempty open
intervals `(x, b(x))` are pairwise disjoint, and picking a rational in each is injective.

*Reduction.* By Mathlib's `HereditarilyLindelofSpace.of_forall_isOpen` it suffices that every
open set is Lindelöf, and for that it suffices that every family `(U j)` of open sets has a
countable subfamily with the same union `V`.

*The two parts of `V`.* Let `W` be the union of the Euclidean interiors of the `U j`. These
interiors are open in `ℝ`, which is hereditarily Lindelöf, so countably many of them have union
`W`. A point `x ∈ V \ W` lies in some `U j(x)`, which contains some `[x, b(x))` with `b(x) > x`;
the Euclidean open interval `(x, b(x))` then lies in the interior of `U j(x)`, hence in `W`. By
the lemma `V \ W` is countable, and the countably many `U j` chosen for `W`, together with the
`U j(x)` for `x ∈ V \ W`, have union `V`.

*Needs:* 1.1; from Mathlib `HereditarilyLindelofSpace.of_forall_isOpen` and the second
countability of `ℝ` (`SecondCountableTopology.toHereditarilyLindelof`).

### 1.3 The Sorgenfrey line is perfectly normal

Prove `t6Space_sorgenfrey`: `Sorgenfrey` is T₀, normal, and every closed set is a Gδ.
(Proved here. Normality is [Ga31], Example 2.)

Route. For `x < y` the open set `[x, y)` contains `x` and not `y`, so the space is T₀. The
clopen sets `[a, b)` form a basis, so a point `x` outside a closed set `F` has a clopen
neighbourhood `[x, y)` missing `F`, whose complement is an open set containing `F`: the space is
regular. It is Lindelöf by 1.2, hence normal by Mathlib's
`NormalSpace.of_regularSpace_lindelofSpace`. For a closed set `F`, its complement is a union of
basic sets `[a, b)`, and by 1.2 countably many of them, `C 0, C 1, …`, suffice. Then `F` is the
intersection of the open sets `(C n)ᶜ`, a Gδ. (If no basic set is needed, the complement is empty
and `F = univ` is open.)

*Needs:* 1.1, 1.2; from Mathlib `NormalSpace.of_regularSpace_lindelofSpace`.

### 1.4 The Sorgenfrey line is paracompact

Prove `paracompactSpace_sorgenfrey`. (Sorgenfrey [So], pp. 631–632, for the non-negative
half-line; [Ga41], Example 4, via the theorem stated there as Theorem 41.5, that a regular
Lindelöf space is paracompact. Proved here by a different route.)

Route. Let `(U j)` be an open cover. Each point `x` lies in a basic clopen set `[x, b)` inside
some `U j`. By 1.2 countably many of these basic sets, `C 0, C 1, …`, cover the line. Put
`D n = C n \ (C 0 ∪ … ∪ C (n − 1))`. Each `D n` is clopen (a finite Boolean combination of clopen
sets), the `D n` are pairwise disjoint, they cover the line, and `D n ⊆ C n` lies in some `U j`.
A pairwise disjoint open cover is locally finite: the member containing a point is a
neighbourhood meeting no other member. The refinement is indexed by `ℕ`, which lies in the
universe of `Sorgenfrey` as Mathlib's `ParacompactSpace` requires.

*Needs:* 1.1, 1.2.

### 1.5 Compact subsets are countable

Prove `countable_of_isCompact_sorgenfrey`: every compact subset `K` of `Sorgenfrey` is
countable. (Proved here.)

Route. Fix `x ∈ K`. The half-line `(−∞, x)` is clopen, so `K ∩ (−∞, x)` is a closed subset of a
compact set, hence compact. It is covered by the increasing open sets `(−∞, x − 1/(n + 1))`, so
one of them contains it: there is `δ(x) > 0` with no point of `K` in `(x − δ(x), x)`. For
`x < y` in `K`, the interval `(y − δ(y), y)` misses `K ∋ x`, so `x ≤ y − δ(y)` and the intervals
`(x − δ(x), x)` and `(y − δ(y), y)` are disjoint. Choosing a rational in each interval injects
`K` into `ℚ`. For `K = ∅` there is nothing to prove.

*Needs:* 1.1.

### 1.6 The Sorgenfrey plane is neither Lindelöf nor paracompact

Prove `not_lindelofSpace_and_not_paracompactSpace_sorgenfrey_prod`:
`¬ LindelofSpace (Sorgenfrey × Sorgenfrey) ∧ ¬ ParacompactSpace (Sorgenfrey × Sorgenfrey)`.
(Non-Lindelöf: [Ga30], Example 4. Non-paracompact: [Ga41], Example 4, and Sorgenfrey [So],
p. 632, for the square of the half-line, with a Baire category argument.)

Route. Let `A` be the antidiagonal `{(x, −x)}`. It is closed: at a point with `x + y > 0` the
box `[x, x + 1) × [y, y + 1)` has all coordinate sums at least `x + y`, and at a point with
`x + y < 0` the box with sides `δ = −(x + y)/2` has all sums below `0`. It is discrete: the box
`[x, x + 1) × [−x, −x + 1)` meets `A` only in `(x, −x)`, since `u ≥ x` and `−u ≥ −x` force
`u = x`. The map `x ↦ (x, −x)` is a bijection from `ℝ` onto `A`, so `#A = 𝔠` (Mathlib's
`Cardinal.mk_real`).

*Not Lindelöf.* If the plane were Lindelöf, the closed set `A` would be Lindelöf
(`IsClosed.isLindelof`) and, being discrete, countable (`IsLindelof.countable`); but
`ℵ₀ < 𝔠` (`Cardinal.aleph0_lt_continuum`).

*Not paracompact.* The line is separable, since every nonempty `[a, b)` contains a rational, so
the plane is separable (Mathlib's instance for products), and Jones' lemma
`IsClosed.not_normal_of_continuum_le_mk`, applied to `A`, shows that the plane is not normal.
This step is the theorem `not_normalSpace_prod` of Mathlib's `Counterexamples/SorgenfreyLine.lean`
for that file's own copy of the line (see Scope); it is re-proved here because that file cannot
be imported. The plane is Hausdorff, since the line is (its topology is finer than that of `ℝ`),
so a paracompact plane would be normal by Dieudonné's theorem
`NormalSpace.of_paracompactSpace_r1Space`.

*Needs:* 1.1; from Mathlib `IsClosed.not_normal_of_continuum_le_mk`, `IsClosed.isLindelof`,
`IsLindelof.countable`, `NormalSpace.of_paracompactSpace_r1Space`, `Cardinal.mk_real`,
`Cardinal.aleph0_lt_continuum`.

### Examples

- The Sorgenfrey line is neither locally compact nor σ-compact. A compact neighbourhood of `x`
  would contain some `[x, y)` with `x < y`, which is uncountable, against 1.5; and a countable
  union of countable compact sets is countable, while the line is not.
- The line is not metrizable and not second countable (both also in Mathlib's
  `Counterexamples/SorgenfreyLine.lean`). A metrizable line would have a metrizable, hence
  paracompact, square, against 1.6; and a second countable regular space is metrizable (Urysohn,
  Mathlib's `metrizableSpace_of_t3_secondCountable`).
- With 1.2 and 1.4, the theorem 1.6 shows that neither the Lindelöf property nor paracompactness
  passes from a space to its square. With 1.3, the non-normality step of its route shows the same
  for normality, and the regularity of the square shows that a regular space need not be normal.
- The identity from the Sorgenfrey line to `ℝ` is continuous and not a homeomorphism: `[0, 1)` is
  open in the line and not in `ℝ`.

### Dependencies

1.2 needs 1.1; 1.3 needs 1.1 and 1.2; 1.4 needs 1.1 and 1.2; 1.5 and 1.6 need 1.1. All Mathlib
inputs are listed in the supplier contracts.

## Layer 2: the Niemytzki plane

### 2.1 Definitions

Define `NiemytzkiPlane` and its topology as in the Conventions. The facts below are used in both
targets.

1. Every Euclidean open subset of the closed half-plane is open. At a point of `E` it contains a
   small disc inside `E`; at a point `(t, 0)` it contains the trace of a Euclidean ball of radius
   `ρ`, which contains `{(t, 0)} ∪ D(t, ρ/2)`, since `D(t, ρ/2)` has diameter `ρ`.
2. The open half-plane `E` is open (a union of discs of the first family), so the axis `L` is
   closed. On `E` the topology is the Euclidean one.
3. For `0 < r ≤ r'`, `D(t, r) ⊆ D(t, r')`, because `|q − (t, 0)|² < 2 r q.2 ≤ 2 r' q.2`. A
   generating set that contains the axis point `(t, 0)` is of the form `{(t, 0)} ∪ D(t, r)`, so
   the sets `{(t, 0)} ∪ D(t, r)` with `r > 0` form a neighbourhood basis at `(t, 0)`.

**Checks.**

- The condition `0 < r` in the second family cannot be dropped. With `r = 0` the disc is empty, the
  singleton `{(t, 0)}` becomes open, and the axis is an open discrete set of the size of the
  continuum. Every dense set then contains the whole axis, so the space is not separable, and 2.3
  fails.

### 2.2 The Niemytzki plane is completely regular

Prove `t35Space_niemytzkiPlane`: `NiemytzkiPlane` is T₀ and completely regular. (Chodounský [Ch],
§1, opening sentence, p. 38, which states complete regularity and non-normality and refers to
Engelking. Proved here.)

Route. *Points are closed.* Two distinct points have neighbourhoods missing each other: small
discs in `E`, a small disc in `E` against a tangent disc of small radius, or two tangent discs at
different axis points whose radii are smaller than a quarter of the distance between the points.
So the space is T₁, hence T₀.

*Separating functions.* Let `K` be closed and `p ∉ K`. Mathlib's class asks for a continuous
`f` into `[0, 1]` with `f p = 0` and `f = 1` on `K`; it is enough to build `g` with `g p = 1` and
`g = 0` on `K`, and take `f = 1 − g`.

If `p ∈ E`, by 2.1.2 there is a Euclidean disc `B(p, ρ) ⊆ E` missing `K`. The map
`q ↦ max(0, 1 − |q − p|/ρ)` is Euclidean continuous, hence continuous by 2.1.1, equal to `1` at
`p` and to `0` off `B(p, ρ)`.

If `p = (t, 0)`, by 2.1.3 some `{p} ∪ D(t, r)` misses `K`. Put `g p = 1`; for `q ∈ E` put
`g q = max(0, 1 − |q − p|²/(2 r q.2))`; and put `g = 0` at the other axis points. Then `g` takes
values in `[0, 1]` and vanishes off `{p} ∪ D(t, r)`, hence on `K`. It is continuous at points
of `E`, which is open with the Euclidean topology, since `q.2 > 0` there. At an axis point
`(t', 0)` with `t' ≠ t` it vanishes on a neighbourhood: the closed Euclidean disc of radius `r`
centred at `(t, r)` meets the axis only at `p`, so its complement is a Euclidean open set
containing `(t', 0)` on which `g = 0`. At `p`, for `0 < ε ≤ 1`, the basic set
`{p} ∪ D(t, ε r)` is the set where `|q − p|² < 2 ε r q.2`, together with `p`, so `g > 1 − ε`
on it. So `g` is continuous.

*Needs:* 2.1.

### 2.3 The Niemytzki plane is separable and not normal

Prove `separableSpace_and_not_normalSpace_niemytzkiPlane`:
`SeparableSpace NiemytzkiPlane ∧ ¬ NormalSpace NiemytzkiPlane`. (Non-normality: Chodounský [Ch],
§1, opening sentence. Separability is proved here; the route to non-normality is Jones' lemma,
Jones [Jo], Theorem 1, p. 671.)

Route. *Separable.* Let `Q` be the countable set of points with rational coordinates and
positive second coordinate. A nonempty open set `U` contains a point `x` and a finite
intersection of generating sets containing `x`. If `x ∈ E`, that intersection meets `E` in a
Euclidean open set containing `x` (2.1.2), which contains a point of `Q`. If `x = (t, 0)`, every
generating set containing `x` is of the form `{x} ∪ D(t, r)`, and by 2.1.3 their intersection
contains the disc `D(t, r)` of smallest radius, a nonempty Euclidean open set inside `E`, which
contains a point of `Q`. So `Q` is dense.

*The axis.* `L` is closed (2.1.2). It is discrete: `{(t, 0)} ∪ D(t, 1)` meets `L` only in
`(t, 0)`. The map `t ↦ (t, 0)` is a bijection from `ℝ` onto `L`, so `#L = 𝔠`. Mathlib's
`IsClosed.not_normal_of_continuum_le_mk` gives `¬ NormalSpace NiemytzkiPlane`.

*Needs:* 2.1; from Mathlib `IsClosed.not_normal_of_continuum_le_mk`, `Cardinal.mk_real`.

### Examples

- The Niemytzki plane is not Lindelöf: a Lindelöf closed discrete set is countable, and `L` is
  not; alternatively, by 2.2 and 2.3 and Mathlib's `NormalSpace.of_regularSpace_lindelofSpace`.
- It is a completely regular space that is not normal, and so it is not metrizable, since
  metrizable spaces are normal. Its open subspace `E` is metrizable, and its closed subspace `L`
  is discrete.
- The plane is first countable: the sets `{(t, 0)} ∪ D(t, 1/(n + 1))` form a neighbourhood
  basis at `(t, 0)` by 2.1.3, and Euclidean discs of rational radius do so at points of `E`.

### Dependencies

2.2 and 2.3 need 2.1. Layer 2 uses nothing from Layer 1.

## Layer 3: closure, interior and complement

Throughout, `α` is a Boolean algebra, `c` a closure operator on it (Mathlib's
`ClosureOperator α`: monotone, `x ≤ c x` and `c (c x) = c x`), `k = c` and `i x = (c xᶜ)ᶜ`.
Then `i` is monotone, `i x ≤ x` and `i (i x) = i x`, since complement reverses the order and is an
involution. The key identities, proved here and used in 3.1 and 3.2, are

    k i k i = k i    and    i k i k = i k.

For the first, put `y = k i x`. From `i y ≤ y` and monotonicity, `k i y ≤ k y = y`. From
`i x ≤ k i x` and monotonicity, `i x = i i x ≤ i k i x`, so `k i x ≤ k i k i x`. The second is
the first applied to `xᶜ` and complemented, since `i = ᶜ ∘ k ∘ ᶜ`.

### 3.1 At most fourteen sets for every closure operator

Prove `encard_closureComplement_le_fourteen`: for every closure operator `c` on a Boolean algebra
and every `a`, the closure–complement orbit of `a` has at most 14 elements. (Kuratowski's theorem
for topological closure; Bowron [Bo], p. 1, and footnote 8, p. 2, which credits Chittenden and
Hammer with dropping the axioms `c ⊥ = ⊥` and additivity. Proved here for this generality.)

Route. Let `W` be the seven words `id, k, i, ki, ik, kik, iki`. Left multiplication by `k` or
`i` maps `W` into itself up to the key identities and idempotence: `k·kik = kik`,
`k·iki = kiki = ki`, `i·kik = ikik = ik`, `i·iki = iki`, and the other cases are immediate. Let
`S` be the set of the elements `w a` and `(w a)ᶜ` for `w ∈ W`; it has at most 14 elements. It
contains `a` and is closed under complement and under `k`, because `k (w a) = (k w) a` and
`k ((w a)ᶜ) = ((i w) a)ᶜ` by the definition of `i`. By induction on `Relation.ReflTransGen` the
orbit lies in `S`.

*Needs:* the key identities above; from Mathlib `ClosureOperator.monotone`,
`ClosureOperator.le_closure`, `ClosureOperator.idempotent`.

**Checks.**

- The closure operators on the subsets of an `n`-point set are the families of subsets closed
  under intersection and containing the whole set: 1, 2, 7, 61, 2480 of them for
  `n = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4`. Over all of them and all subsets, the largest closure–complement orbit has
  1, 2, 4, 8, 10 elements and the largest closure–interior orbit 1, 2, 3, 5, 5.
- On a one-point Boolean algebra (the empty type's subsets) the orbit is `{⊥}` with one element,
  since `⊥ = ⊤ = ⊥ᶜ`.

### 3.2 At most seven sets for closure and interior

Prove `encard_closureInterior_le_seven`: the orbit of `a` under the moves `x ↦ c x` and
`x ↦ (c xᶜ)ᶜ` has at most 7 elements. (For topological closure: Bowron [Bo], §2.1, p. 2, where
the seven operators of `W` form the set `K0`, and footnote 8, p. 2, which records the identity
`bibi = bi`. Proved here for every closure operator.)

Route. The set `{w a : w ∈ W}` has at most 7 elements, contains `a`, and is closed under `k` and
`i` by the multiplication table in 3.1; induction on `Relation.ReflTransGen` as before.

*Needs:* the key identities above; from Mathlib `ClosureOperator.monotone`,
`ClosureOperator.le_closure`, `ClosureOperator.idempotent`.

### 3.3 At most ten sets in an extremally disconnected space

Prove `encard_closureComplement_le_ten`: if the closure of every open set of `X` is open
(Mathlib's `ExtremallyDisconnected`, with no separation axiom), then the closure–complement orbit
of every `s : Set X` has at most 10 elements. (Bowron [Bo], Table 4, p. 9, gives 10 as the order
of the closure–complement monoid of the extremally disconnected type, and Corollary 14, p. 33,
glosses extremally disconnected spaces as those satisfying `ibi = bi`. The bound for each single
set is proved here.)

Route. Write `k = closure` and `i = interior`. The set `i s` is open, so `k i s` is open and
`i k i s = k i s`. The set `i k s` is open, so `k i k s` is open; it lies in `k k s = k s`, and an
open subset of `k s` lies in `i k s`; with `i k s ⊆ k i k s` this gives `k i k s = i k s`. The
five words `id, k, i, ki, ik` are then closed under left multiplication by `k` and `i`
(`k·ik = kik = ik`, `i·ki = iki = ki`, the rest by idempotence), and the argument of 3.1 bounds
the orbit by the ten sets `w s`, `(w s)ᶜ`.

*Needs:* the route of 3.1; from Mathlib `ExtremallyDisconnected.open_closure`,
`interior_compl`, `closure_compl`.

**Checks.**

- Of the 1, 1, 4, 29, 355 topologies on `n = 0, …, 4` points, 1, 1, 4, 26, 255 are extremally
  disconnected; their largest orbits have 1, 2, 4, 6, 8 elements.
- The hypothesis cannot be dropped: the real line is not extremally disconnected (the closure
  `[0, 1]` of `(0, 1)` is not open), and it has a set with an orbit of 14 (Examples below).

### Examples

- In a discrete space every orbit is `{s, sᶜ}`; in a nonempty space the orbit of `∅` is
  `{∅, univ}`.
- For `c = Topology.closureOperator X`, Mathlib's closure operator of a topological space, 3.1
  and 3.2 are Kuratowski's bounds for closure, interior and complement.
- The bound 14 is attained in `ℝ` (Bowron [Bo], p. 1, for the existence of such a set). Mathlib's
  `Archive/Examples/Kuratowski.lean` proves that `(0, 1) ∪ (1, 2) ∪ {3} ∪ ([4, 5] ∩ ℚ)` has exactly
  fourteen sets in its orbit, so this roadmap does not restate it. For this set the closure–interior
  orbit has 7 elements, so 3.2 is also sharp in `ℝ`. Both counts were recomputed in an exact model
  of the subsets of `ℝ` built from the points `0, …, 5` and the open intervals between them, each
  interval marked empty, full, rational or irrational.

### Dependencies

3.1 and 3.2 use the key identities at the head of the layer; 3.3 uses the route of 3.1. Layer 3
uses nothing from Layers 1 and 2.

## Scope and ownership

Owned here: the types `Sorgenfrey` and `NiemytzkiPlane` with their topologies, and the ten
theorems 1.2–1.6, 2.2, 2.3 and 3.1–3.3.

Already in the library and cited, not restated: Jones' lemma in the form
`IsClosed.not_normal_of_continuum_le_mk`, the normality of regular Lindelöf spaces and of
paracompact Hausdorff spaces, Mathlib's closure operators and its closure operator of a
topological space.

Mathlib's repository also has a `Counterexamples` and an `Archive` folder. They are built as
separate libraries and cannot be imported from the roadmap's environment, so nothing here uses
them as suppliers; what they prove is not restated as a target.

- `Counterexamples/SorgenfreyLine.lean` (Yury Kudryashov) defines the Sorgenfrey line as a type
  synonym of `ℝ` with the topology generated by the intervals `[a, b)`, keeping the order and
  field structure of `ℝ`. It proves that the line is completely normal, first countable,
  separable and totally disconnected, with continuous addition and an order-closed topology; that
  the square is not normal (`not_normalSpace_prod`), its antidiagonal being closed, discrete, of
  the size of the continuum and not separable as a subspace, with the rational and irrational
  points of the antidiagonal not separated by neighbourhoods; and that the line is neither
  metrizable nor second countable. The non-normality of the square is a step in the route of
  1.6 here. The file has no statement on the Lindelöf property, perfect normality,
  paracompactness or compact subsets.
- `Counterexamples/Omega1Space.lean` (Juanjo Madrigal) proves that the product of `[0, ω₁)` and
  `[0, ω₁]` with the order topologies is not normal, an example not treated here.
- `Archive/Kuratowski.lean` (Jan Förster, Leon Müller, Luis Sand, Junyan Xu) proves that the sets
  obtained from a subset of a topological space by closure and complement number at most 14,
  counted with `Set.ncard`, by the identity `kckckck = kck` for topological closure.
  `Archive/Examples/Kuratowski.lean` (the same authors) proves that the subset
  `(0, 1) ∪ (1, 2) ∪ {3} ∪ ([4, 5] ∩ ℚ)` of `ℝ` gives exactly 14. Here 3.1 states the bound for
  every closure operator on a Boolean algebra, 3.2 and 3.3 are bounds that file does not treat,
  and the sharpness in `ℝ` is only recalled in the Examples of Layer 3.

Outside Lean, the Isabelle Archive of Formal Proofs has an entry on Kuratowski's theorem, and
Mizar has a Sorgenfrey line; both are context only. Nothing in this roadmap is taken from these
developments, and each layer is designed on Mathlib's classes.

Not treated: the Tychonoff plank, the Michael line, Bing's and Dieudonné's examples, the
closure–complement–boundary monoid of Gaida and Eremenko, the classification of spaces by their
Kuratowski monoids, and set-theoretic refinements of Jones' lemma.
