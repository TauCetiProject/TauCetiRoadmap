# Roadmap: highest weight theory, part II — sl₂-triples, weight filtrations and weight patterns

**Summary.** Over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, attach to every `sl₂`-triple of a Lie algebra, the zero triple included, an increasing filtration on each finite-dimensional module, and prove that in a finite-dimensional semisimple Lie algebra the dimensions of these filtrations, taken over all modules, determine the triple up to inner automorphism. In the classical types the standard module suffices except for very even partitions in type `D`, where one half-spin module separates the two classes; in the exceptional types the adjoint module suffices, by a finite comparison of root profiles carried out directly in each of `G₂`, `F₄`, `E₆`, `E₇`, `E₈`. A second strand proves the highest-weight half-integrality test and shows that the geometric weight patterns of a semisimple element of a connected complex reductive group determine its positive real part up to conjugacy. It is Part II of LieHighestWeight. That roadmap, on top of the highest weight theory that Tau Ceti already proves (`sl₂` theory, weights, Verma modules, the classification of finite-dimensional irreducibles, complete reducibility, characters and Chevalley restriction), develops the split Serre presentation and the exceptional models, and stops short of `sl₂`-triples of a general semisimple algebra, their filtrations and their conjugacy, where this roadmap begins. The end theorems are those used by Ciubotaru–Harris (Lemmas 2.4, 6.30, 10.9 and Proposition 10.2): filtration recognition over a semisimple Lie algebra, its classical and exceptional refinements, and recognition of the positive real part.

## Conventions

`K` is an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, except in §§5.3–5.5, where the field is `ℂ`, and in the Lie-algebra statement of §5.2, which needs only characteristic zero and a splitting Cartan subalgebra. `L` is a Lie algebra over `K`; it is finite-dimensional with nondegenerate Killing form (`LieAlgebra.IsKilling`) wherever conjugacy is discussed, which over `K` is semisimplicity. Modules are Lie modules over `K`; "representation" means a finite-dimensional Lie module unless an algebraic representation of a group is named. Half-spin modules are Lie modules of `so(2r)`; nothing here asks them to descend to a special orthogonal group. Over `K` they are realised on the even and odd exterior algebras `S⁺ = ⋀^even W`, `S⁻ = ⋀^odd W` of a maximal isotropic subspace `W` (Tau Ceti `TauCeti.spinPlus`, `TauCeti.spinMinus`) as modules of the split algebra `LieAlgebra.Orthogonal.typeD`, and `Suggested.lean` states the type-`D` recognition theorem on that split carrier.

A triple is an ordered `(h, e, f)` with `[e, f] = h`, `[h, e] = 2e`, `[h, f] = −2f`; `h` is its neutral element and `e` its nilpositive element. The zero triple `(0, 0, 0)` is a triple in this sense. Mathlib's `IsSl2Triple` adds `h ≠ 0`; in characteristic zero the two notions differ exactly by the zero triple (§0.1).

Conjugacy of triples means conjugacy under `Int(L)`, Tau Ceti's `TauCeti.intLieAlgebra K L`: the subgroup of `LinearMap.GeneralLinearGroup K L` generated by `exp(ad x)` for ad-nilpotent `x ∈ L`, acting on `L` by evaluation. The exponential series divides by factorials and is read through a `ℚ`-Lie algebra structure on `L`; the Lean statements carry it as an instance argument `[LieAlgebra ℚ L]`, as Tau Ceti does. Such a structure exists (`TauCeti.ratLieAlgebra`, by restricting scalars along `ℚ → K`) and is unique, since an additive group carries at most one `ℚ`-module structure, so the argument names a structure rather than choosing one. For a connected semisimple algebraic group `G` over `K` with a fixed isomorphism `Lie(G) ≅ L`, it is conjugacy under `Ad(G(K))` (§0.2).

For a triple `t = (h, e, f)` and a module `M` write `M_j = ker(ρ(h) − j)` for `j ∈ ℤ` and `W_i(M) = Σ_(j ≤ i) M_j`. The filtration is increasing and `e` raises the index by two: `ρ(e) W_i ⊆ W_(i+2)`. Write `d_i = dim W_i − dim W_(i−1) = dim M_i` (the graded weight multiplicities) and, for `n ≥ 0`, `m_n = d_n − d_(n+2)` (the multiplicity of the standard module `V(n)` of dimension `n + 1`). Three integer sequences are in play — the cumulative dimensions `dim W_i`, the graded multiplicities `d_i` and the `sl₂` multiplicities `m_n` — and they are never identified: for the adjoint module of `sl₂` they are `1, 1, 2, 2, 3` (at `i = −2, …, 2`), `1, 0, 1, 0, 1` and `m_2 = 1`. Each `W_i` is a subspace, generally not an `L`-submodule. Reindexing `Fil^n = W_(−n)` gives a decreasing filtration; the ordering used throughout is the increasing one of Ciubotaru–Harris (CH Definition 2.2, p.6, CH line 288).

Exceptional root systems use Bourbaki numbering with Cartan entries `A_ij = ⟨α_i, α_j∨⟩`, as pinned by `TauCeti.DynkinType.cartanMatrix`, and roots are written in coordinates on the simple roots. `G₂` has `A = [[2, −1], [−3, 2]]` (`α₁` short); `F₄` has `A₂₃ = −2`, `A₃₂ = −1` (`α₁, α₂` long); the `E_r` edges are `(1,3), (3,4), (2,4), (4,5), …, (r−1, r)`. The opposition involution `−w₀` exchanges `1 ↔ 6` and `3 ↔ 5` in `E₆` and is trivial for `G₂, F₄, E₇, E₈`. Mathlib's `CartanMatrix.G₂` is the transpose of this matrix; the isomorphism class of `Matrix.ToLieAlgebra` does not depend on the choice.

For `so(2r)` use coordinates `ε₁, …, ε_r` on a Cartan subalgebra, simple roots `ε_i − ε_(i+1)` (`i < r`) and `ε_(r−1) + ε_r`, so that the dominant chamber is `a₁ ≥ ⋯ ≥ a_(r−1) ≥ |a_r|` and `ω_r = ½(ε₁ + ⋯ + ε_r)`; this is the convention of SpinRepresentations Layer 5. The half-spin module `L(ω_r)` has as weights the vectors `½(±1, …, ±1)` with an even number of minus signs.

In Layer 5, `q > 1` is a fixed real number and absolute values are the usual ones on `ℂ`. A semisimple element `s` of a connected complex reductive group is `q`-geometric if in every finite-dimensional algebraic representation every eigenvalue of `s` has absolute value `q^(i/2)` for some `i ∈ ℤ` (the complex form of CH Definition 2.5, p.7, CH line 323). The functionals `ν` of §§5.1–5.2 are additive maps to `ℝ` on a group of weights (a lattice `X`, or the dual `H^*` of a Cartan subalgebra in the Lie-algebra statement of §5.2); on a lattice `X` they are exactly the real linear functionals on `X ⊗ ℝ`, and no ordering of `K` is used. In `Suggested.lean` a connected complex reductive group is a finite-type commutative Hopf `ℂ`-algebra `H` with Tau Ceti's reductivity property `TauCeti.reductiveCommHopfAlgProperty`, its group of `ℂ`-points `G(ℂ)` is the convolution group `WithConv (H →ₐ[ℂ] ℂ)`, its finite-dimensional algebraic representations are the finitely generated `H`-comodules, and `q`-geometric is the predicate `IsGeometricPoint q s` of §5.3.

The quantifier over modules in the recognition theorem ranges over a universe containing `K` and `L`, so that the adjoint module and every `Kⁿ` occur.

## How to read the build

Layer 0 fixes the objects: zero-inclusive triples, the inner automorphism group and its comparison with the adjoint group, invariance of modules under inner twists, and conjugacy of triples from their neutral elements. Layer 1 builds the weight filtration of a triple on every module and proves that its dimensions are exactly the data of the restricted `sl₂`-module, with exactness and tensor and dual laws. Layers 2 and 3 are independent of each other: Layer 2 identifies the classical matrix algebras with their types and recognises triples in them by the standard module and one half-spin module, Layer 3 recognises them in the exceptional algebras by the adjoint module, through dominant characteristics and a finite injectivity statement for root profiles. Layer 4 assembles the semisimple recognition theorem over simple factors. Layer 5 is independent of Layers 1–4: it proves the highest-weight half-integrality test from the highest-weight theory of Tau Ceti and of ReductiveGroups §10B, defines weight patterns and positive real parts for connected complex reductive groups, and proves recognition of the positive real part from the character separation of ReductiveGroupsPartIII.

## Layer 0: zero-inclusive triples and inner conjugacy

The zero triple is part of every statement: it is the triple of the zero nilpotent element, and its filtration is concentrated in degree zero (CH §2.1, p.6, CH line 277).

### 0.1 Zero-inclusive triples

Define `IsSl2TripleOrZero h e f` as the conjunction of `[e, f] = h`, `[h, e] = 2e` and `[h, f] = −2f`, with natural-number scalars as in `IsSl2Triple`, and no condition on `h`. Over a field of characteristic zero, `IsSl2TripleOrZero h e f` holds if and only if `(h, e, f) = (0, 0, 0)` or `IsSl2Triple h e f`: if `h = 0` then `2e = 0` and `2f = 0`, so `e = f = 0` because `2` is invertible (CH §2.1, relations (2.1), p.6, CH line 279).

Prove the following about `IsSl2TripleOrZero`.

- The zero triple satisfies the predicate; a triple with `h ≠ 0` satisfies it exactly when it satisfies `IsSl2Triple`.
- In characteristic zero, `h = 0` forces `e = f = 0`, and `e = 0` forces `h = 0` and then `f = 0`.
- A Lie algebra homomorphism `φ` carries zero-inclusive triples to zero-inclusive triples, `(φh, φe, φf)`; identity and composition act as expected. A non-injective `φ` can carry a nonzero triple to the zero triple.
- Negation and exchange: `(−h, f, e)` is a zero-inclusive triple whenever `(h, e, f)` is.
- A zero-inclusive triple determines, through `TauCeti.lieHomOfSl2Basis`, a Lie homomorphism `sl(2, K) → L` sending the standard basis to `(e, f, h)`; it is zero exactly for the zero triple.

**Checks.**

- `(0, 0, 0)` satisfies the predicate but not `IsSl2Triple`, whose field `h ≠ 0` fails; a definition that only renamed `IsSl2Triple` would exclude the zero nilpotent element.
- In `sl(2, K)` the standard `H = diag(1, −1)`, `E = E₁₂`, `F = E₂₁` satisfy it: `[E, F] = H`, `[H, E] = 2E`, `[H, F] = −2F`.
- `(0, E, 0)` fails: `[0, E] = 0 ≠ 2E`. A central nonzero `h` with `e = f = 0` fails `[e, f] = h`. Exchanging `E` and `F` while keeping `H` fails `[F, E] = −H ≠ H`; `(−H, F, E)` satisfies it.
- Over a ring with `2 = 0`, `(0, e, f)` satisfies the relations `[h, e] = 2e` and `[h, f] = −2f` for all `e, f`, and the remaining relation asks exactly `[e, f] = 0`: in `sl(2, 𝔽₂)`, `(0, E₁₂, 0)` is a nonzero solution (all three brackets and both doubled elements vanish), while `(0, E₁₂, E₂₁)` is not, since `[E₁₂, E₂₁] = diag(1, 1) ≠ 0`. The characteristic-zero hypothesis is what makes the zero branch a single triple.

*Needs:* Mathlib `IsSl2Triple`; Tau Ceti `TauCeti.lieHomOfSl2Basis`.

### 0.2 Inner automorphisms, the adjoint group and inner twists

Let `G` be a connected semisimple algebraic group over `K` with an isomorphism `Lie(G) ≅ L`. Prove that the image of `Ad : G(K) → Aut(L)` equals `Int(L)`. For an ad-nilpotent `x ∈ L`, `x` acts nilpotently in a faithful representation `G ↪ GL(V)` (`TauCeti.isNilpotent_toEnd_of_isNilpotent_ad`), the finite exponential `exp(tx)` preserves the defining Hopf ideal of `G` (the invariant-derivation argument of ReductiveGroups Layer 5P), and differentiating the adjoint action along this one-parameter subgroup (ReductiveGroups Layer 2) gives `Ad(exp x) = exp(ad x)`; so `Int(L) ⊆ Ad(G(K))`; conversely `G(K)` is generated by its root subgroups `U_α(K) = exp(K e_α)`: by the Bruhat decomposition `G(K) = ⋃_w B(K) n_w B(K)` with `B = T U⁺` (ReductiveGroups Layer 7), `U⁺` and `U⁻` are generated by root subgroups, each `n_w` is a product of the elements `n_α(1) = x_α(1) x_(−α)(−1) x_α(1)` for simple `α`, and `T` is generated by the coroot images `α∨(c) = n_α(c) n_α(1)⁻¹`: for semisimple `G` the centre `⋂_α ker α` is finite (ReductiveGroups Layer 6), so the roots span `X(T) ⊗ ℚ`, the coroots span `Y(T) ⊗ ℚ` (ReductiveGroups Layer 7, root datum), and the image of the homomorphism of tori `∏_α G_m → T`, `(c_α) ↦ ∏_α α∨(c_α)`, is a closed connected subgroup of full dimension, hence `T`. The `SL₂` Check below computes the rank-one factorisation `diag(c, c⁻¹) = w(c) w(1)⁻¹` that this uses. The adjoint images of the root subgroups are the generators `exp(t ad e_α)` of `Int(L)`. In particular the image does not depend on the isogeny class of `G`; directly, a central isogeny `G → G'` is surjective on `K`-points, as every surjective morphism of varieties over an algebraically closed field is, and its central kernel acts trivially under `Ad` (ReductiveGroups Layer 6).

Prove inner invariance of modules: for every finite-dimensional `L`-module `M`, with `L` finite-dimensional Killing, and every `φ ∈ Int(L)`, the twisted module `M^φ` (action `x · m = φ(x)m`) is isomorphic to `M`. For a generator `φ = exp(ad x)`, `ρ(x)` is nilpotent (`TauCeti.isNilpotent_toEnd_of_isNilpotent_ad`) and `exp(ρ(x))` intertwines: `exp(ρ x) ρ(y) exp(−ρ x) = ρ(exp(ad x) y)`, by `TauCeti.expAd_apply_eq_exp_mul_exp_neg` in `End(M)` after applying `ρ` to `exp(ad x)y`. Products of intertwiners handle products of generators. This holds for every module, including those that do not integrate to the chosen `G` (CH Appendix, Lemma 10.9, p.35, CH line 1703, quantifies over all modules of the Lie algebra).

Prove the following about `Int(L)` and inner twists.

- `Int(L)` consists of Lie automorphisms (`TauCeti.intLieAlgebraLieEquiv`); it is generated by `exp(ad x)` for `x` ad-nilpotent, and `exp(ad x)⁻¹ = exp(ad(−x))`.
- For a triple `(h, e, f)` with `e, f` ad-nilpotent, `TauCeti.weylAut` lies in `Int(L)` (`IsSl2Triple.ofLinearEquiv_weylAut_mem_intLieAlgebra`) and sends `h` to `−h`; so `h` and `−h` are `Int(L)`-conjugate.
- Inner invariance: for `φ ∈ Int(L)` and a triple `t`, the filtrations of `t` and of `φ(t)` on `M` have the same dimensions in every degree.
- For a connected semisimple `G` with `Lie(G) ≅ L`, `Ad(G(K))`-conjugacy of triples is `Int(L)`-conjugacy.

**Checks.**

- For `L = sl(2, K)` and `G = SL₂`, `Ad(diag(c, c⁻¹))` acts on `E` by `c²`. With `x(t) = exp(tE)`, `y(t) = exp(tF)` and `w(c) = x(c) y(−c⁻¹) x(c) = [[0, c], [−c⁻¹, 0]]`, one has `diag(c, c⁻¹) = w(c) w(1)⁻¹`, so this automorphism is a product of generators `exp(t ad E)`, `exp(t ad F)` of `Int(sl₂)`; `Ad(−I)` is the identity, matching `Ad(SL₂(K)) = PGL₂(K) = Int(sl₂)`.
- For an abelian `L` (not Killing) every element is ad-nilpotent with `exp(ad x) = id`, so `Int(L)` is trivial, while `exp(ρ(x))` need not exist; the Killing hypothesis in inner invariance is what makes `ρ(x)` nilpotent.
- For `so(8)` and `G = SO(8)`, the half-spin module `L(ω₄)` does not descend to `SO(8)`, yet inner invariance applies to it: `exp(ρ(x))` is defined from the Lie action alone.
- An outer automorphism need not preserve filtration dimensions on a fixed module: the automorphism of `so(8)` induced by a reflection in `O(8)` exchanges `L(ω₃)` and `L(ω₄)`.

*Needs:* ReductiveGroups Layers 2, 5P, 6, 7 (Bruhat decomposition, root datum, centre of a semisimple group) and §7C (rank-one maps); Tau Ceti `TauCeti.intLieAlgebra`, `TauCeti.intLieAlgebraLieEquiv`, `IsSl2Triple.ofLinearEquiv_weylAut_mem_intLieAlgebra`, `TauCeti.expAd`, `TauCeti.expAd_apply_eq_exp_mul_exp_neg`, `TauCeti.isNilpotent_toEnd_of_isNilpotent_ad`, `TauCeti.weylAut`.

### 0.3 Triple conjugacy from the neutral element

Let `L` be finite-dimensional Killing over `K`. Two zero-inclusive triples `(h, e, f)` and `(h', e', f')` are `Int(L)`-conjugate if and only if `h` and `h'` are `Int(L)`-conjugate. The zero triple is conjugate only to itself. Consequently a triple is determined up to conjugacy by the dominant representative of its neutral element in a fixed Cartan subalgebra (§3.1), its characteristic.

For a zero-inclusive triple in a finite-dimensional `L` over `K`, with `L_j = ker(ad h − j)`, `ad e` maps `L_0` onto `L_2`, and in every finite-dimensional module `ρ(e)` maps `M_j` onto `M_(j+2)` for integers `j ≥ −1` and injectively for `j ≤ −1`. For a nonzero triple these are Tau Ceti's `IsSl2Triple.map_ad_e_eigenspace_zero`, `IsSl2Triple.map_toEnd_e_eigenspace` and `IsSl2Triple.disjoint_ker_toEnd_e_eigenspace`, over every field of characteristic zero. For the zero triple `ρ(e) = 0` and `M = M_0`: the image of `L_0 = L` is `0 = L_2`, every `M_(j+2)` with `j ≥ −1` is zero, and every `M_j` with `j ≤ −1` is zero. (DG §3, proof of Lemma 3, p.4, DG line 151; over `ℂ` this is NilpotentOrbits N2.2.)

A proof can proceed as follows. Reduce to `h = h'` (the zero case is immediate since `h = 0` forces `e = f = 0`). Let `L_j` be the `ad h`-eigenspaces, so that `[L_0, e] = L_2`. Let `G` be a connected semisimple group over `K` with an isomorphism `Lie(G) ≅ L`, for instance the split adjoint group of `L`: choose a Cartan subalgebra `H` (`LieAlgebra.exists_isCartanSubalgebra_engel`; it is split as `K` is algebraically closed) and take the adjoint root datum of the root system `LieAlgebra.IsKilling.rootSystem H`, with the root lattice as character lattice; ReductiveGroups §9B constructs its split group over `ℤ` and base-changes it to `K`, connected and semisimple because the roots span the character lattice (ReductiveGroups Layers 6, 7, 9D); item 1 of ReductiveGroups §9A identifies its Lie algebra with the pinned Lie algebra of the root datum; and the split Serre presentation of LieHighestWeight Layer 7 identifies that pinned Lie algebra with `L`, both being finite-dimensional Killing Lie algebras over `K` with the root system of `(L, H)`. Let `G_h` be the centralizer of `h`, with `Lie(G_h) = L_0` (ReductiveGroups Layers 2–3); the orbit map `G_h → L_2`, `g ↦ Ad(g)e`, has surjective differential `u ↦ [u, e]` at the identity, so in characteristic zero its image is open and dense in `L_2` (DG §3, Lemma 3, p.4, DG line 143). The same holds for `e'`; two dense open orbits in the irreducible `L_2` meet, so `Ad(g)e = e'` for some `g ∈ G_h(K)`, and `Ad(g) ∈ Int(L)` by §0.2. Finally `f` is determined by `h` and `e`: two choices differ by an element of `ker(ad e) ∩ L_(−2)`, which is zero (`IsSl2Triple.disjoint_ker_toEnd_e_eigenspace`, `−2` not being a natural number). (DG §3, Lemmas 2–3, p.4, DG line 139; SOM §3.1, pp.15–16, SOM line 364.)

**Checks.**

- In `sl(2, K)` the dominant neutral elements are `0` and `H`; they give the two classes, the zero triple and the standard triple.
- In `sl(3, K)` the neutral elements `diag(1, 0, −1)` and `diag(2, 0, −2)` belong to the minimal and the regular nilpotent classes; they are not conjugate (their eigenvalue sets differ), and neither are the triples.
- In `so(8)` the two very even classes of partition `(4, 4)` are exchanged by the automorphism induced by an element of `O(8)` of determinant `−1`; their neutral elements are not `Int(so(8))`-conjugate, and the theorem keeps the two classes apart (§2.3). Replacing `Int(L)` by the full automorphism group would merge them.
- Equality of `h` alone is necessary but `e` is not determined by `h` as an element: in `sl(2, K)`, `(H, cE, c⁻¹F)` is a triple for every `c ≠ 0`, and these are conjugate by `Ad(diag(c^(1/2), c^(−1/2)))`.

*Needs:* §0.1; §0.2; ReductiveGroups Layers 2, 3, 6, 7, 9D and §§9A (item 1), 9B; LieHighestWeight Layer 7 (split Serre presentation); Mathlib `LieAlgebra.exists_isCartanSubalgebra_engel`, `LieAlgebra.IsKilling.rootSystem`; Tau Ceti `IsSl2Triple.map_ad_e_eigenspace_zero`, `IsSl2Triple.map_toEnd_e_eigenspace`, `IsSl2Triple.disjoint_ker_toEnd_e_eigenspace`; NilpotentOrbits N2.2 as the `ℂ` comparison only.

### Examples

In `sl(2, K)` there are exactly two classes of zero-inclusive triples, represented by `(0, 0, 0)` and `(H, E, F)`. The Weyl automorphism `exp(ad E) exp(−ad F) exp(ad E)` sends `(H, E, F)` to `(−H, −F, −E)`, a triple conjugate to the standard one, and the neutral element `−H` is conjugate to `H`.

## Layer 1: representation filtrations and graded multiplicities

### 1.1 Restriction along a triple

A zero-inclusive triple `t = (h, e, f)` in `L` gives the Lie homomorphism `sl(2, K) → L` of §0.1; pulling a module `M` back along it gives the restricted `sl₂`-module `M|_t`, on which `H, E, F` act by `ρ(h), ρ(e), ρ(f)`. For the zero triple every element of `sl₂` acts by zero (CH Appendix, proof of Lemma 10.9, p.35, CH line 1707).

Prove the following about restriction.

- The restricted actions of `H, E, F` are `ρ(h), ρ(e), ρ(f)`; restriction is the pullback of Lie modules along a homomorphism, not a second module carrier.
- An `L`-module map is an `sl₂`-module map between the restrictions; identities and composition are preserved.
- Along the identity of `sl(2, K)` with its standard triple, restriction is the identity.
- For the zero triple the restriction is a direct sum of `dim M` trivial modules.

**Checks.**

- A two-dimensional trivial `L`-module restricts, for any triple, to two copies of the trivial `sl₂`-module.
- The standard module `K²` of `sl(2, K)` restricts along the standard triple to `V(1)`, with `H`-weights `1` and `−1`; along `(−H, F, E)` it restricts to a module isomorphic to `V(1)` by the coordinate swap.
- The adjoint module of `sl(3, K)` restricted along the minimal triple (`h = diag(1, 0, −1)`) has `H`-weights `2, 1, 1, 0, 0, −1, −1, −2`, so it is `V(2) ⊕ 2V(1) ⊕ V(0)`; restricted along the zero triple it is `8V(0)`.

*Needs:* §0.1; Mathlib `LieModule.toEnd`; Tau Ceti `TauCeti.lieHomOfSl2Basis`.

### 1.2 The increasing weight filtration

Define `weightFiltration K h M i` as the sum over the integers `j ≤ i` of the eigenspaces `ker(ρ(h) − j)`, a subspace of `M` (CH §2.2, Definition 2.2, p.6, CH line 288). For a zero-inclusive triple on a finite-dimensional module over `K` the family is increasing, zero for `i` sufficiently negative and equal to `M` for `i` sufficiently positive; with the inclusions it is an object of `CategoryTheory.FilteredObject` indexed by `ℤ` on the underlying module `M`.

Prove the following about `weightFiltration`.

- For `i ≤ k`, the `i`-th step is contained in the `k`-th; for the neutral element of a zero-inclusive triple and finite-dimensional `M`, the step is `0` for `i < −N` and `M` for `i ≥ N`, where `N` is the largest element of `{0} ∪ {|j| : M_j ≠ 0}`, so `N = 0` for `M = 0`, whose eigenvalue set is empty.
- The successive quotient at `i` is the eigenspace `M_i` (§1.3); the eigenspaces at non-integers are zero (`TauCeti.exists_int_of_hasEigenvalue` for `h ≠ 0`, whose Noetherian and torsion-free hypotheses hold for a finite-dimensional space over `K`; directly for `h = 0`).
- `ρ(e)` maps the `i`-th step into the `(i+2)`-th and `ρ(f)` maps it into the `(i−2)`-th; `ρ(h)` preserves every step and acts by `i` on the `i`-th successive quotient (the defining properties of an `SL(2)`-type filtration, CH Definition 2.2, p.6, CH line 291). On the graded pieces `ρ(e) : M_j → M_(j+2)` is surjective for `j ≥ −1` and injective for `j ≤ −1` (§0.3, from `IsSl2Triple.map_toEnd_e_eigenspace` and `IsSl2Triple.disjoint_ker_toEnd_e_eigenspace`). In particular every nonzero `ρ(h)`-eigenvector killed by `ρ(e)` has a nonnegative integer eigenvalue; and the whole kernel is the sum of its intersections with the `M_n`, `n ∈ ℕ` (`IsSl2Triple.ker_toEnd_e_eq_iSup_inf_eigenspace` for a nonzero triple; for the zero triple `ker ρ(e) = M = M_0`), hence `ker ρ(e) ⊆ ⨁_(j ≥ 0) M_j`. A vector of the kernel need not itself be an eigenvector.
- Every `L`-module map `M → M'` sends the `i`-th step of `M` into the `i`-th step of `M'`; identities and composition are preserved.
- For the zero triple the step is `0` for `i < 0` and `M` for `i ≥ 0`.
- For `φ ∈ Int(L)` the filtrations of `φ(t)` and `t` on `M` have equal dimensions (§0.2).

**Checks.**

- For the one-dimensional trivial module the only jump is at `0`, whatever the triple.
- For the zero module every eigenspace is `0`, so `ρ(h)` has no eigenvalue and a bound read as "the largest absolute value of an eigenvalue" is undefined; with `N = 0` every step is `0 = M`, meeting both bounds. For `V(1)` the bound is `N = 1`, for `V(2)` it is `N = 2`, and for a nonzero trivial module `N = 0`.
- For `V(1)` along the standard triple, `dim W_i` is `0` for `i < −1`, `1` for `−1 ≤ i < 1` and `2` for `i ≥ 1`.
- For the adjoint module of `sl₂`, `dim W_(−2) = 1`, `dim W_(−1) = 1`, `dim W_0 = 2`, `dim W_1 = 2`, `dim W_2 = 3`; the graded multiplicities are `1, 0, 1, 0, 1`. A definition by the decreasing convention `Fil^i = Σ_(j ≥ i) M_j` gives `3, 2, 2, 1, 1` instead.
- Each step is generally not a submodule: in the adjoint module of `sl₂`, `W_(−2) = K F` is not stable under `ad E`.
- The kernel statement concerns eigenvectors, not arbitrary vectors. In `V(0) ⊕ V(1)` along the standard triple, with `u` spanning `V(0)` and `v₊`, `v₋` the weight `1` and `−1` vectors of `V(1)` (`ρ(e)v₋ = v₊`), the vector `x = u + v₊` satisfies `ρ(e)x = 0`, but `ρ(h)x = v₊` is not a multiple of `x`: the coefficient of `u` forces the eigenvalue `0` and that of `v₊` forces `1`. Its components have the nonnegative weights `0` and `1`, so `x ∈ M_0 ⊕ M_1`, while `v₋`, of weight `−1`, is not killed.

*Needs:* §0.1; §1.1; Mathlib `Module.End.eigenspace`, `CategoryTheory.FilteredObject`; Tau Ceti `TauCeti.exists_int_of_hasEigenvalue`, `TauCeti.iSup_eigenspace_toEnd_intCast_eq_top`, `IsSl2Triple.map_toEnd_e_eigenspace`, `IsSl2Triple.disjoint_ker_toEnd_e_eigenspace`, `IsSl2Triple.ker_toEnd_e_eq_iSup_inf_eigenspace`.

### 1.3 Graded pieces and recovery of the restricted sl₂-module

Let `t` be a zero-inclusive triple and `M` finite-dimensional. The inclusion of `M_i` into the `i`-th step induces an isomorphism `M_i ≅ W_i / W_(i−1)`. Hence `d_i = dim M_i`, `d_i = d_(−i)`, `d_i` has finite support, and for `n ≥ 0` the multiplicity of `V(n)` in `M|_t` is `m_n = d_n − d_(n+2) ≥ 0`; conversely `d_i = Σ m_n` over `n ≥ |i|` with `n ≡ i (mod 2)`.

Prove `finrank_weightFiltration_eq_iff_exists_intertwiner`: for zero-inclusive triples `t` in `L` and `t'` in `L'` and finite-dimensional modules `M`, `M'`, the filtrations have equal dimensions in every degree if and only if some linear isomorphism `M ≅ M'` intertwines `ρ(h), ρ(e), ρ(f)` with `ρ'(h'), ρ'(e'), ρ'(f')`, that is, `M|_t ≅ M'|_(t')` (CH Appendix, proof of Lemma 10.9, p.35, CH line 1708).

A proof can proceed as follows. Split the `i`-th step as the `(i−1)`-th plus `M_i` using the internal direct sum `TauCeti.isInternal_eigenspace_toEnd_intCast`. In `V(n)` each weight `n, n−2, …, −n` has multiplicity one, so after decomposing by `TauCeti.Sl2Std.exists_isInternal_lieModuleEquiv` the number of summands `V(n)` is `d_n − d_(n+2)`; nonnegativity is the count of actual summands, not a property of natural-number subtraction. Equal cumulative dimensions give equal `d_i`, equal `m_n`, and isomorphic restrictions; the converse holds because an intertwiner preserves eigenspaces.

**Checks.**

- For `V(2) ⊕ 2V(0)`: `d_0 = 3`, `d_2 = d_(−2) = 1`, `m_0 = 2`, `m_2 = 1`, and the cumulative `dim W_0 = 4`.

### 1.4 Exactness, tensor products and duals

For a zero-inclusive triple, the assignment `M ↦ (M, W(M))` is exact and compatible with tensor products and duals. Every module map is strict: `φ(W_i M) = φ(M) ∩ W_i M'`. A short exact sequence `0 → M' → M → M'' → 0` stays exact on every step. `W_i(M ⊗ N) = Σ_(a+b=i) W_a M ⊗ W_b N`, equivalently the sum over `a + b ≤ i`. For the contragredient dual, `W_i(M*)` is the annihilator of `W_(−i−1)(M)`. The tensor unit has its only jump at `0`. The comparison maps are the identity on underlying tensors and the evaluation `M* ⊗ M → K`, so the associativity, symmetry, unit and evaluation constraints are respected (CH §2.2, Definition 2.2, p.6, CH line 288, where the filtration is a symmetric monoidal functor).

A proof can proceed as follows. A module map commutes with `ρ(h)` and so respects eigenspaces; injectivity and surjectivity are checked on each eigenspace, which gives strictness and exactness. On `M ⊗ N` the neutral element acts by `ρ(h) ⊗ 1 + 1 ⊗ ρ(h)`, so eigenvalues add, and on the contragredient `M* = M →ₗ[K] K` it acts by `−ρ(h)ᵀ`, so they change sign (the tensor-product and linear-map Lie modules of `Mathlib/Algebra/Lie/TensorProduct.lean` and `Mathlib/Algebra/Lie/Basic.lean`); sum the eigenspaces. No claim that all filtered vector spaces form an abelian category is used.

**Checks.**

- `V(1) ⊗ V(1)` has graded multiplicities `1, 2, 1` at `−2, 0, 2`, so it is `V(2) ⊕ V(0)`; its cumulative dimensions are `1, 1, 3, 3, 4` at `i = −2, …, 2`.
- For `M = V(1)`, `W_(−1)(M*)` is the annihilator of `W_0(M) = K v_(−1)`, a line, and `W_0(M*)` is the annihilator of `W_(−1)(M) = K v_(−1)`; writing `W_(−i)` in place of `W_(−i−1)` would give the annihilator of `W_1(M) = M` at `i = −1`, the zero space, which is wrong.
- For the unit `K`, `W_i(K*) = Ann(W_(−i−1) K)` is `0` for `i < 0` and `K` for `i ≥ 0`, so duality fixes the filtration of the unit.

## Layer 2: classical types

Throughout, `V` is finite-dimensional over `K`; in the orthogonal and symplectic cases `B` is a nondegenerate symmetric, respectively alternating, bilinear form on `V`, and the Lie algebra is `so(V, B)`, respectively `sp(V, B)`. The type `A` classification of nilpotent matrices by partitions is NilpotentOrbits N4; it is used here through §1.3 only.

### 2.0 The classical matrix algebras and their types

For `r ≥ 1` the split classical algebras `sl(r+1, K)`, `sp(2r, K)` (Mathlib's `LieAlgebra.Symplectic.sp (Fin r) K`) and `so(2r+1, K)`, and for `r ≥ 2` the algebra `so(2r, K)`, are finite-dimensional Killing Lie algebras in which the diagonal matrices (for `so`, after the identification with the split models `LieAlgebra.Orthogonal.typeB`, `LieAlgebra.Orthogonal.typeD`) form a split Cartan subalgebra, with root systems `A_r`, `C_r`, `B_r` and `D_r` in the coordinates of the Conventions: the roots are `ε_i − ε_j`; `±ε_i ± ε_j` (`i ≠ j`) and `±2ε_i`; `±ε_i ± ε_j` (`i ≠ j`) and `±ε_i`; and `±ε_i ± ε_j` (`i ≠ j`) (KIR Appendix C, §§C.1–C.4, pp.124–129, KIR lines 4760, 4888, 4835, 4958; semisimplicity KIR Theorem 6.33, p.75, KIR line 2919, which asks `n > 2` for `so(n)`). They are simple except for `so(4) ≅ sl₂ × sl₂` (`r = 2`), which is semisimple and not simple; `so(2r, K)` is simple for `r ≥ 3`. The case `r = 1` of the even orthogonal family is excluded: `so(2)` is a nonzero abelian algebra, so its Killing form is zero and it is not Killing. `so(1)`, `so(0)`, `sl(1)`, `sl(0)` are zero. Consequently, by the split Serre presentation of LieHighestWeight Layer 7, a simple Killing Lie algebra over `K` whose root system has type `A_r` (`r ≥ 1`), `B_r` (`r ≥ 2`), `C_r` (`r ≥ 3`) or `D_r` (`r ≥ 4`), the rank ranges of `TauCeti.DynkinType.Valid`, is isomorphic to `sl(r+1, K)`, `so(2r+1, K)`, `sp(2r, K)` or `so(2r, K)`. Here `so(n, K)` is `LieAlgebra.Orthogonal.so n K` for any index type `n` of the given cardinality; it is identified with `LieAlgebra.Orthogonal.typeB` and `LieAlgebra.Orthogonal.typeD` by `LieAlgebra.Orthogonal.typeBEquivSo'`, `LieAlgebra.Orthogonal.typeDEquivSo'` and `LieAlgebra.Orthogonal.soIndefiniteEquiv` (`K` contains a square root of `−1`), restricted along `Matrix.reindexLieEquiv` for the reindexing. The matrix groups `SL_(r+1)`, `Sp_(2r)` and `SO_n` (`n ≥ 3`) are connected semisimple algebraic groups over `K` with these Lie algebras (ReductiveGroups Layers 2 and 6; connected reductivity is Tau Ceti `TauCeti.SpecialOrthogonal.reductiveCommHopfAlgProperty_finiteTypeCoordinateHopfAlgebra` and `TauCeti.Symplectic.reductiveCommHopfAlgProperty_finiteTypeCoordinateHopfAlgebra`, and a connected reductive group whose Lie algebra is Killing has finite centre), so that §0.2 applies to them.

A proof can proceed as follows. For `sl(r+1, K)` the Killing property is `TauCeti.instHasTrivialRadicalSl` with Mathlib `LieAlgebra.HasTrivialRadical.instIsKilling`; the trace-zero diagonal matrices, the intersection with `sl` of the diagonal Cartan subalgebra `TauCeti.diagonalCartan` of `gl_(r+1)`, form a splitting Cartan subalgebra whose root vectors are the matrix units `E_ij`, `i ≠ j`, of weight `ε_i − ε_j`, which is the root system `A_r`. For `sp`, `LieAlgebra.Orthogonal.typeB` and `LieAlgebra.Orthogonal.typeD` the matrix root bases, Killing nondegeneracy and simplicity are ReductiveGroups §9A (item 9A.1b), the folded models inside `sl`, after scalar extension from `ℚ`; `LieAlgebra.Orthogonal.typeB ι K` is Killing by `TauCeti.isKilling_typeB`, and for `LieAlgebra.Orthogonal.typeD (Fin n) K`, `n ≥ 4`, they are `TauCeti.TypeDStd.isKilling_typeD`, `TauCeti.TypeDStd.isSimple_typeD` and the Serre system of `TauCeti.TypeDStd.lieBasis`. The abstract identification composes the split Serre presentations of both sides (LieHighestWeight Layer 7), each isomorphic to `Matrix.ToLieAlgebra` of the same transposed Cartan matrix. The lower ranks are the coincidences of the Checks, which `TauCeti.DynkinType.Valid` names once.

### 2.1 The explicit invariant forms on the standard sl₂-modules

On `V(n)` (Tau Ceti `TauCeti.Sl2Std K n`, of dimension `n + 1`) take the basis `w_j = ρ(f)^j w_0`, `j = 0, …, n`, generated from a highest weight vector `w_0`, so that `w_j` has weight `n − 2j` and `ρ(e) w_j = j(n − j + 1) w_(j−1)`; in the basis `v_j` of KIR Theorem 5.6, equation (5.4), `w_j = j! v_j` (KIR Theorem 5.6, p.61, KIR line 2369). Define `β_n` as the bilinear form on `V(n)` with `β_n(w_j, w_(n−j)) = (−1)^j` and `β_n(w_j, w_k) = 0` for `j + k ≠ n`; its Gram matrix in the basis `w_0, …, w_n` is anti-diagonal with entries `1, −1, …, (−1)^n` read from the top row down. The form depends on the chosen highest weight vector `w_0` only through a nonzero scalar: replacing `w_0` by `c w_0`, hence every `w_j` by `c w_j`, multiplies `β_n` by `c^(−2)`.

Prove the following about `β_n`.

- `β_n` is `sl₂`-invariant: `β_n(ρ(x)u, w) + β_n(u, ρ(x)w) = 0` for `x ∈ {h, e, f}` and all `u, w ∈ V(n)`. On basis vectors this is a direct computation from the action of KIR Theorem 5.6, (5.4): under `h` the weights `n − 2j` and `n − 2k` cancel exactly when `j + k = n`; under `f` the two terms at `j + k + 1 = n` are `(−1)^(j+1) + (−1)^j = 0`; under `e` the two terms at `j + k − 1 = n` both carry the factor `j(n − j + 1)` with opposite signs.
- `β_n` is nondegenerate: the anti-diagonal Gram matrix has determinant `1`, the sign `(−1)^(n(n+1)/2)` of the order-reversing permutation of the `n + 1` indices cancelling the product `Π_j (−1)^j = (−1)^(n(n+1)/2)` of the entries.
- `β_n` is symmetric when `n` is even and alternating when `n` is odd: on the Gram matrix `β_n(w_k, w_j) = (−1)^n β_n(w_j, w_k)`, since `(−1)^(n−j) = (−1)^n (−1)^j`, and for odd `n` no `w_j` pairs with itself.
- Every `sl₂`-invariant bilinear form on `V(n)` is a scalar multiple of `β_n`. This is consumed from Tau Ceti `TauCeti.LieModule.exists_eq_smul_of_lieInvariant` (on a finite-dimensional irreducible Lie module over an algebraically closed field every invariant form is a multiple of any nonzero invariant one), applied with the nonzero invariant `β_n`, `V(n)` being irreducible by `TauCeti.Sl2Std.isIrreducible`.

**Checks.**

- On `V(1) = K²`, `β_1` has matrix `[[0, 1], [−1, 0]]` in the basis `w_0, w_1`: it is the determinant pairing, alternating, with transpose `−β_1`.
- On `V(2)`, `β_2` is anti-diagonal with entries `(1, −1, 1)`, symmetric. Identified with the adjoint module by `w_0 = E`, `w_1 = [F, E] = −H`, `w_2 = −2F`, the trace form `tr(xy)` takes the values `tr(E · (−2F)) = −2`, `tr(H²) = 2`, `−2` on the anti-diagonal, so it is `−2β_2`.
- The symmetric form with `δ_jk` in the basis `w_j` of `V(1)` is not invariant: invariance under `h` at `(w_0, w_0)` asks `1 + 1 = 0`. An invariant form pairs only opposite weights.
- A form invariant under `H` alone is not determined up to scalar: on `V(2)` any form pairing `w_0, w_2` and `w_1, w_1` with independent values is `H`-invariant, and only the `E, F` conditions tie these values together.

### 2.2 Orthogonal and symplectic partitions

For `L = so(V, B)` or `sp(V, B)` and a zero-inclusive triple `t`, the restricted standard module decomposes as `⨁_(n ≥ 0) V(n) ⊗ U_n`, and `B` restricts to `β_n ⊗ γ_n` with `γ_n` a nondegenerate form on `U_n`, of the opposite symmetry type to `β_n` in the symplectic case and of the same type in the orthogonal case. Hence `e` has Jordan blocks of size `n + 1` with multiplicity `dim U_n`; in the orthogonal case every even block size has even multiplicity, in the symplectic case every odd block size has even multiplicity. Two triples of `so(V, B)` (respectively `sp(V, B)`) are conjugate under `O(V, B)(K)` (respectively `Sp(V, B)(K)`) if and only if their restrictions to `V` are isomorphic. An `O(V, B)`-class splits into two `SO(V, B)`-classes exactly when `dim V > 0` and the partition is very even (every block size even, every multiplicity even); otherwise it is one `SO(V, B)`-class. For `V = 0` the empty partition satisfies both evenness conditions vacuously, but `O(0) = SO(0) = 1` and the zero triple forms one class. (Over `ℂ`: MAT Proposition 2.1.1, p.2, MAT lines 66–69, for the parity conditions; MAT p.2, MAT lines 70–74, and CH Appendix, proof of Lemma 10.9, p.35, CH lines 1716–1718, for the splitting of very even classes. The proof here uses §2.1.)

A proof can proceed as follows. Distinct isotypic summands are `B`-orthogonal by Schur's lemma, and on `V(n) ⊗ U_n` the form factors through §2.1. Over `K` nondegenerate symmetric or alternating forms are classified by dimension, and alternating forms exist only in even dimension; this gives the parity conditions, existence, and uniqueness up to `O` or `Sp`. For the splitting, an element of the orthogonal centralizer acting by a reflection on a symmetric `U_n` has determinant `(−1)^(n+1)` on `V(n) ⊗ U_n`; a symmetric `U_n` with `n` even exists exactly when an odd block size occurs. If all block sizes are even, every element of the orthogonal centralizer has determinant `1`; when `dim V > 0`, `O` contains a reflection, so `SO` has index two in `O` and the class splits into two. When `dim V = 0`, `SO = O` and there is one class.

**Checks.**

- In `sp(4)`, the partition `(3, 1)` is excluded (odd parts `3` and `1` each of multiplicity one) while `(2, 2)` occurs.
- In `so(8)`, `(4, 4)` and `(2, 2, 2, 2)` are very even and split into two `SO(8)`-classes; `(3, 3, 1, 1)` is not very even and is a single class.
- In `so(4) ≅ sl₂ × sl₂`, `(2, 2)` is very even: its two classes are the triples supported in one or the other factor, while `(3, 1)` is the diagonal triple.
- In `so(3) ≅ sl₂` the only partitions are `(3)` and `(1, 1, 1)`; `(2, 1)` is excluded because the even part `2` has odd multiplicity.
- For `V = 0` the empty partition is very even in the combinatorial sense, yet `O(0) = SO(0)` is trivial and there is exactly one class, the zero triple; a splitting criterion without `dim V > 0` would predict two. In dimension `4` the very even `(2, 2)` gives two classes (the check on `so(4)` above).

### 2.3 Half-spin separation of very even classes

Let `L = so(2r)`, `r ≥ 2`, with the coordinates of the Conventions, and let `λ` be a very even partition of `2r`; then `r` is even. The neutral elements of the two `SO(2r)`-classes with partition `λ` are, in the dominant chamber, `h_I = (a₁, …, a_(r−1), a_r)` and `h_II = (a₁, …, a_(r−1), −a_r)` with odd `a₁ ≥ ⋯ ≥ a_r > 0` (the `r` positive `h`-eigenvalues on `V`, with multiplicity). On the half-spin module `L(ω_r)`, whose weights are the vectors `½(±1, …, ±1)` with an even number of minus signs, the largest index `i` with `W_i ≠ W_(i−1)` is `A = ½(a₁ + ⋯ + a_r)` for `h_I` and `A − a_r` for `h_II`. Hence the two classes have different filtrations on `L(ω_r)`. (CH Appendix, proof of Lemma 10.9, p.35, CH lines 1716–1727.)

The ordering of coordinates matters. CH lists the simple roots as `±ε₁ + ε₂, ε₂ − ε₃, …` together with ascending coordinates `a₁ ≤ ⋯ ≤ a_r`, which are not dominant for `ε₂ − ε₃` (for `(1, 1, 3, 3)` the pairing with `ε₂ − ε₃` is `−2`); with ascending coordinates the simple roots must be `±ε₁ + ε₂, ε₃ − ε₂, …, ε_r − ε_(r−1)`. The convention fixed above reverses the coordinates instead, and the maxima are unchanged.

A proof can proceed as follows. The value of `h` on the weight `½(s₁, …, s_r)` is `½ Σ s_i a_i`. For `h_I` the maximum over even sign vectors is at all signs `+`. For `h_II` the all-`+` vector gives `A − a_r`; the vector with one minus sign at `r` is odd and excluded; any even vector with a minus sign at some `i < r` and at `r` gives `A − a_i ≤ A − a_r`. Pass from different top indices to different filtrations by §1.3. Over `K` the half-spin modules are realised explicitly: for a polarisation `K^(2r) = W ⊕ W'` into maximal isotropic subspaces (`TauCeti.SpinPolarizationData` with `line = ⊥`), `so(2r) ≅ LieAlgebra.Orthogonal.typeD (Fin r) K` acts on the exterior algebra `⋀W` through the Clifford algebra, and the even and odd parts `S⁺ = ⋀^even W` (`TauCeti.spinPlus`) and `S⁻ = ⋀^odd W` (`TauCeti.spinMinus`) are submodules (`TauCeti.SpinPolarizationData.typeDSpinPlusLieRep`, `TauCeti.SpinPolarizationData.typeDSpinMinusLieRep`), `S⁺` irreducible (`TauCeti.SpinPolarizationData.isIrreducible_typeDSpinPlusLieRep`). The diagonal Cartan subalgebra acts on the exterior basis vector `e_S`, `S ⊆ {1, …, r}`, by the sign vector with `+½` on `S` and `−½` off `S` (`TauCeti.SpinPolarizationData.typeDSpinLieRep_apply_cartan_exteriorBasis`), so the weights of `S⁺` are the sign vectors with an even number of minus signs when `r` is even and an odd number when `r` is odd, those of `S⁻` the others, each with multiplicity one (`TauCeti.SpinPolarizationData.formalCharacter_typeDSpinLieRep` for the full spinor module). For even `r ≥ 4` the top vector `e_{1…r}` lies in `S⁺` and is a highest weight vector of weight `ω_r` (`TauCeti.SpinPolarizationData.isHighestWeightVector_typeDSpinPlusLieRep_exteriorBasis_univ_of_even`), so `S⁺ ≅ L(ω_r)` by the classification of irreducible modules by highest weights (`TauCeti.nonempty_lieModuleEquiv_of_isHighestWeightVector`, `S⁺` being irreducible) and the weights of `L(ω_r)` are the even sign vectors; for `r = 2` the same holds by direct computation in `so(4) ≅ sl₂ × sl₂`, where `S⁺` is the standard module of one factor. This agrees with `ω_r` being minuscule in the sense of `TauCeti.IsMinuscule`, its weights forming the single `W(D_r)`-orbit of `ω_r`: the Weyl group of `D_r`, the signed permutations with an even number of sign changes, is transitive on the even sign vectors, and `⟨ω_r, α∨⟩ ∈ {0, ±1}` for every root (the Check below). Over `ℂ` this is the half-spin module of SpinRepresentations Layer 5.

**Checks.**

- In `so(8)`, partition `(4, 4)`: the `h`-eigenvalues on `V` are `3, 3, 1, 1, −1, −1, −3, −3`, so `a = (3, 3, 1, 1)`, and the maxima on `L(ω₄)` are `4` and `3`.
- In `so(8)`, partition `(2, 2, 2, 2)`: `a = (1, 1, 1, 1)`, maxima `2` and `1`.
- On the standard module `V` both neutral elements have the same eigenvalues, so the standard filtrations agree; a test family consisting of `V` and its tensor powers cannot separate the classes, because the outer automorphism exchanging them fixes the class of `V`.
- Using the odd half-spin module `L(ω_(r−1))` exchanges the roles: the maxima become `A − a_r` for `h_I` and `A` for `h_II`; either half-spin module separates.
- The full spinor module `S⁺ ⊕ S⁻ = ⋀W` does not separate: its top index is `A` for both `h_I` and `h_II`, because the sign vector with its only minus sign at `r` is odd and lies in `S⁻`; a test family using the whole Clifford module instead of one parity summand would miss the very even classes.

`T` is one of `G₂, F₄, E₆, E₇, E₈`, of rank `r`, with root set `Φ_T ⊂ ℤ^r` in simple-root coordinates for the Bourbaki-numbered Cartan matrix `TauCeti.DynkinType.cartanMatrix` (RootSystems §4.1).

### 3.1 Necessary conditions on a characteristic

Let `L` be simple with a fixed Cartan subalgebra `H` and base `{α_i}`, and `t` a zero-inclusive triple. Some `Int(L)`-conjugate of `h` lies in `H` and is dominant, and it is unique; its labels `a_i = α_i(h)` form the characteristic of `t`. For a nonzero triple the dominant representative is an element of Tau Ceti's `RootPairing.Base.dominantNeutralElements` and the characteristic is its `RootPairing.Base.weightedDynkinDiagram`, read in `{0, 1, 2}`; that set excludes `0` (`RootPairing.Base.zero_notMem_dominantNeutralElements`), and the zero triple, whose neutral element is `0`, has characteristic `0`. The characteristic satisfies: each `a_i ∈ {0, 1, 2}`; `a` is fixed by the permutation of `−w₀`; and the adjoint graded multiplicities `d_m = dim L_m` satisfy `d_m = d_(−m)` and `d_m ≥ d_(m+2)` for every `m ≥ 0`.

A proof can proceed as follows. For the zero triple `h = 0` lies in `H` with all labels `0`. Otherwise the neutral element is ad-semisimple with integer eigenvalues, `L` being the direct sum of the integer eigenspaces of `ad h` (Tau Ceti `TauCeti.isInternal_eigenspace_toEnd_intCast`); so `h` lies in a Cartan subalgebra `H'` of `L` (`TauCeti.exists_isCartanSubalgebra_mem_of_isSemisimple_ad`, `K` being infinite), and `H'` is `Int(L)`-conjugate to `H` (`LieSubalgebra.exists_map_intLieAlgebraLieEquiv_eq`). `IsSl2Triple.exists_mem_intLieAlgebra_smul_mem_dominantNeutralElements` then carries the image of `h` into the dominant chamber by an element of `Int(L)` preserving `H`: the roots take integer values at a neutral element, some element of the Weyl group makes such an element dominant, and that element acts on `H` as an element of `Int(L)` (`TauCeti.exists_mem_intLieAlgebra_forall_smul_eq_coweightEquiv`). The dominant representative is unique: two elements of `H` conjugate under `Int(L)` take the same value under every `L`-invariant polynomial function on `L`, since `Int(L)` fixes these (`TauCeti.SymmetricAlgebra.lift_eval_smul_of_mem_intLieAlgebra`); the restrictions of these functions are all the `W`-invariant polynomials on `H` (Chevalley restriction, `LieSubalgebra.map_lieInvariants_lieCartanRestriction`); and `W`-invariant polynomials separate the orbits of the finite group `W` on the points of `H` (`FixedPoints.forall_mem_subalgebra_eq_iff`). So the two are `W`-conjugate and, both being dominant, equal: all their root values are integers, so both lie in the `ℚ`-span of the coroots, on which `W` acts through the rational form `RootPairing.restrictScalarsRat` with roots and coroots exchanged, and `TauCeti.eq_of_mem_orbit_of_mem_dominantChamber` applies to that root system over the ordered field `ℚ`. Every simple root takes a value in `{0, 1, 2}` at a dominant neutral element of a nonzero triple: the simple-root values are natural numbers by dominance and integrality, `H` is split because `K` is algebraically closed, and Tau Ceti's `IsSl2Triple.root_apply_eq_zero_or_eq_one_or_eq_two` gives the bound (SOM §3.1, p.15, SOM line 373; DG §2, p.3, DG line 87; over `ℂ` this is NilpotentOrbits N2.2). Since `h` is conjugate to `−h` (§0.2), and `−w₀` sends the dominant `−h`-representative back to the chamber, because it permutes the simple roots (`TauCeti.opposition_mem_support`), uniqueness gives `a = −w₀ a`. The inequalities are `m_m = d_m − d_(m+2) ≥ 0` for the adjoint module (§1.3). (SOM §3.1, p.15, SOM line 373; DG §2, p.3, DG line 86.)

**Checks.**

### 3.2 Adjoint root profiles

For `a ∈ ℤ^r` define the profile `D_T(a) : ℤ → ℕ` by `D_T(a)_m = r·[m = 0] + #{c ∈ Φ_T : Σ_i c_i a_i = m}`. If `a` is the characteristic of a triple in the simple algebra of type `T`, then `D_T(a)_m = dim ker(ad h − m)` for every `m`: each root space is a line on which `h` acts by `Σ c_i a_i`, and `H`, of dimension `r`, lies in the zero-weight space and contributes `r` to it; the zero-weight space also contains every root space with `Σ c_i a_i = 0`, so at `a = 0` it is all of `L` (root spaces are lines by Mathlib `LieAlgebra.IsKilling.finrank_rootSpace_eq_one`; DG §2, p.3, DG line 83).

Prove the following about `D_T`.

- `Σ_m D_T(a)_m = r + |Φ_T| = dim L`, the sum being finite.
- `D_T(a)_(−m) = D_T(a)_m`, by `c ↦ −c` on `Φ_T`.

For nonnegative labels `B_T(a) = Σ_i a_i θ_i`, and for labels in `{0, 1, 2}` it is at most `2·ht(θ)`, that is `10, 22, 22, 34, 58`.
- For a characteristic, `D_T(a)` is the graded adjoint multiplicity function, and the cumulative adjoint filtration is `Σ_(j ≤ i) D_T(a)_j`.
- The root set `Φ_T` is the closure of the simple roots under the simple reflections `c ↦ c − (Σ_i c_i A_ij) α_j` (RootSystems §4.1), and has `12, 48, 72, 126, 240` elements (`TauCeti.DynkinType.numRoots`).

### 3.3 Candidate characteristics and injectivity of profiles

Define the candidate set `C_T ⊂ {0, 1, 2}^r` as the set of `a` that are fixed by `−w₀` and satisfy `D_T(a)_m ≥ D_T(a)_(m+2)` for all `m ≥ 0`. By §3.1 every characteristic of a triple lies in `C_T`. Prove that `D_T` is injective on `C_T`, for each `T` separately: if `a, b ∈ C_T` and `D_T(a) = D_T(b)` then `a = b`. The statement is about the finite set `C_T` and asserts nothing about which candidates are characteristics. (CH Appendix, proof of Lemma 10.9, p.35, CH line 1728, asserts the separation for the realised characteristics; the candidate set is a superset that makes the statement independent of the classification of orbits.)

Prove the following about `C_T`.

- `a ∈ C_T` if and only if every `a_i ∈ {0, 1, 2}`, `a` is fixed by `−w₀`, and `D_T(a)_m ≥ D_T(a)_(m+2)` for `0 ≤ m ≤ 2·ht(θ)`; beyond that range both sides vanish (§3.2), so the finitely many inequalities are equivalent to all of them.

### 3.4 Exceptional adjoint separation

## Layer 4: semisimple recognition

### 4.1 Filtration recognition

Prove `exists_innerAut_iff_finrank_weightFiltration`: for a finite-dimensional semisimple `L` over `K` and zero-inclusive triples `t, t'`, the following are equivalent: (1) `t` and `t'` are `Int(L)`-conjugate; (2) for every finite-dimensional `L`-module `M` and every `i ∈ ℤ`, `dim W_i^t(M) = dim W_i^(t')(M)`; (3) for every such `M` the restrictions `M|_t` and `M|_(t')` are isomorphic. In (2) it suffices to test the pullbacks, along the projections to the simple ideals, of the standard module of each classical factor, one half-spin module of each factor of type `D`, and the adjoint module of each exceptional factor. (CH Lemma 2.4, p.6, CH line 296; CH Appendix, Lemma 10.9, p.35, CH line 1701.)

A proof can proceed as follows. (1) ⇒ (2) is inner invariance (§0.2); (2) ⇔ (3) is §1.3. For (2) ⇒ (1), decompose `L = L₁ ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ L_k` into simple ideals, the atoms of its lattice of ideals (Mathlib `LieAlgebra.IsKilling.instSemisimple`, `LieAlgebra.IsSemisimple.isSimple_of_isAtom`), so that `Int(L) = Int(L₁) × ⋯ × Int(L_k)` and each triple decomposes componentwise. Each simple ideal is identified, according to the type of its root system (`TauCeti.existsUnique_dynkinType`), with its matrix model `sl`, `sp` or `so` (§2.0) or with an exceptional Serre algebra (LieHighestWeight Layers 7 and 8). Pull back a module of one factor along its projection: the other ideals act by zero, and the hypothesis gives that factor's recognition hypothesis, classical (§2.4, with the half-spin module `S⁺` of §2.3 for a factor of type `D`) or exceptional (§3.4). The product of the factor conjugators lies in `Int(L)`. Pullback modules are factor-selective, so isomorphic simple factors are never exchanged. The case `L = 0` has only the zero triple.

**Checks.**

- For `L = sl₂ × sl₂`, the triples `(t, 0)` and `(0, t)` are exchanged by the swap automorphism, which is outer; the pullback of the standard module of the first factor separates them (filtration jumps `1, 1` at `±1` versus `2` at `0`).
- For `L = so(8)` the half-spin test is legitimate even when the group in view is `SO(8)` or its adjoint form: the quantifier is over `L`-modules.
- For `L = 0`, `Int(L)` is trivial, the only triple is zero, and every module test holds trivially.
- For `L = sl₂ × sl₂` the adjoint module of `L` does not separate `(t, 0)` from `(0, t)`: both have adjoint weights `2, 0, −2, 0, 0, 0`. The test family must consist of factor-selective pullbacks, not of the adjoint module of `L`.

*Needs:* §0.2; §1.3; §2.0; §2.3; §2.4; §3.4; LieHighestWeight Layers 7 and 8; Mathlib `LieAlgebra.IsKilling.instSemisimple`, `LieAlgebra.IsSemisimple.isSimple_of_isAtom`; Tau Ceti `TauCeti.intLieAlgebra`, `TauCeti.existsUnique_dynkinType`.

## Layer 5: highest weights and weight patterns

### 5.1 Half-integrality on a root-lattice coset

Let `X` be an abelian group, `Q ⊆ X` the subgroup generated by finitely many elements `α_i`, `ν : X → ℝ` additive and `λ ∈ X`. If `ν(α_i) ∈ ½ℤ` for every `i`, then for every `μ ∈ λ + Q`, `ν(μ) ∈ ½ℤ` if and only if `ν(λ) ∈ ½ℤ`. More generally the statement holds with any additive subgroup of `ℝ` in place of `½ℤ`. Here `ν(x) ∈ ½ℤ` means `2ν(x) ∈ ℤ`. (CH §6.12, proof of Lemma 6.30, p.24, CH line 1163.)

**Checks.**

- For `A₁` with `α = 2ω` and `ν(α) = ½`, `ν(ω) = ¼`: the weights `±ω` of the standard module have `ν = ±¼ ∉ ½ℤ`, matching `ν(ω) ∉ ½ℤ`; the adjoint weights `α, 0, −α` have values in `½ℤ`, matching `ν(α) ∈ ½ℤ`.
- Without the hypothesis on the `α_i` the statement fails: with `ν(α) = ⅓`, `λ = 0` and `μ = α`, `ν(λ) ∈ ½ℤ` but `ν(μ) ∉ ½ℤ`.
- With `Q = 0` the coset is `{λ}` and the statement is trivial.

*Needs:* none beyond additive groups.

### 5.2 The highest-weight half-integrality test

Let `K` be a field of characteristic zero, `L` a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over `K` with nondegenerate Killing form, `H` a splitting Cartan subalgebra and `{α_i}` a base of the root system `LieAlgebra.IsKilling.rootSystem H`; algebraic closure of `K` is not used here. Let `ν : H^* → ℝ` be additive with `ν(α_i) ∈ ½ℤ` for every simple root. Prove `forall_weight_halfIntegral_iff_highestWeight`: if an irreducible `L`-module `M`, finite-dimensional or not, has a highest weight vector of weight `λ` (`TauCeti.IsHighestWeightVector`), then every weight `μ` of `M`, that is every linear form `μ` on `H` with `genWeightSpace M μ ≠ 0`, satisfies `ν(μ) ∈ ½ℤ` if and only if `ν(λ) ∈ ½ℤ`; no dominance of `ν` is assumed (CH §6.12, Lemma 6.30, p.24, CH line 1158). For finite-dimensional `M`, `λ` is dominant integral (`TauCeti.IsHighestWeightVector.isDominantIntegral`) and `M ≅ L(λ)` (`TauCeti.nonempty_lieModuleEquiv_of_isHighestWeightVector`), which is the setting of CH. Only the values of `ν` on `λ` plus the root lattice enter. For finite-dimensional `M` these lie in the integral weight lattice `X ⊂ H^*`, whose additive maps to `ℝ` are the real linear functionals on `X ⊗ ℝ`; each extends additively to `H^*`, `ℚ`-linearly from the `ℚ`-span of `X`, on which the fundamental weights are a `ℚ`-basis. So the statement with additive `ν` on `H^*` contains the one with real functionals on the weight space.

The same holds for an irreducible algebraic representation of a connected reductive group over `K`, with maximal torus `T`, character lattice `X = X*(T)`, a base `{α_i}` of its roots and highest weight `λ ∈ X`, for every additive `ν : X → ℝ` with `ν(α_i) ∈ ½ℤ`, again with no dominance of `ν` (CH §6.12, Lemma 6.30, p.24, CH line 1158). This form is not stated in `Suggested.lean`: the pins have no highest weight of an irreducible comodule relative to a Borel subgroup.

A proof can proceed as follows. A nonzero vector generates an irreducible module (`TauCeti.lieSpan_singleton_eq_top_of_ne_zero`), so for every weight `μ` of `M` the difference `λ − μ` lies in the additive monoid `TauCeti.posRootCone` generated by the simple roots (`TauCeti.sub_mem_posRootCone_of_genWeightSpace_ne_bot_of_isHighestWeightVector_of_lieSpan_eq_top`). On that monoid `2ν` takes integer values, by induction over sums of simple roots, so §5.1 for the coset `λ + Q` gives one direction; `λ` is itself a weight (`TauCeti.IsHighestWeightVector.genWeightSpace_ne_bot`), which gives the other. For an irreducible algebraic representation of highest weight `λ`, every weight lies in `λ − Σ ℕα_i` and `λ` occurs (ReductiveGroups §10B); apply §5.1 in the same way. For a torus there are no roots, and the statement is the case `Q = 0` of §5.1.

**Checks.**

- Irreducibility is needed. For `L = sl₂` and `M = V(1) ⊕ V(0)`, a vector `u` spanning `V(0)` is a highest weight vector of weight `0`: it is nonzero, `H` acts on it by `0` and `E` kills it. With `ν(α) = ½`, `ν(0) = 0 ∈ ½ℤ`, but the weights `±ω` of `V(1)` have `ν = ±¼ ∉ ½ℤ`; so the right side holds and the left side fails.
- The hypothesis on the simple roots is needed: for `L = sl₂` with `ν(α) = ⅓`, so `ν(ω) = ⅙`, and `M = V(3)`, `2ν(3ω) = 1 ∈ ℤ`, while the weight `ω` of `V(3)` has `2ν(ω) = ⅓ ∉ ℤ`.
- Infinite-dimensional modules are covered. For `L = sl₂` and `ν(α) = ½`, the Verma module of highest weight `λ` with `λ(H) = −2` is irreducible, since its raising operator sends `F^k v` to `k(λ(H) − k + 1) F^(k−1) v = −k(k+1) F^(k−1) v ≠ 0` for `k ≥ 1`. Its weights `λ − kα = −(k+1)α` have `ν = −(k+1)/2 ∈ ½ℤ`, matching `ν(λ) = −½`. For `λ(H) = −1` the weights `−ω − kα` have `ν = −¼ − k/2 ∉ ½ℤ`, matching `ν(−ω) = −¼`: both sides fail.
- For `G = PGL₂` the character lattice is `ℤα`; with `ν(α) = ½` every algebraic highest weight `λ = kα` has `ν(λ) = k/2 ∈ ½ℤ`, so every weight of every algebraic representation of `PGL₂` is half-integral, whereas the Lie module `L(ω)` of `sl₂`, which is not algebraic for `PGL₂`, has `ν(ω) = ¼`.
- For `L = sl₂`, `λ = 3ω` and `ν(α) = ½`: `ν(λ) = ¾ ∉ ½ℤ`, and indeed `ν(ω) = ¼ ∉ ½ℤ` for the weight `ω`.
- Strengthening the hypothesis to `ν(ω_i) ∈ ½ℤ` for all fundamental weights makes both sides true whenever `λ` lies in the integral weight lattice, the `ℤ`-span of the `ω_i`, in particular for finite-dimensional `M`, since every weight `λ − Σ n_i α_i` then lies in that lattice; it is not assumed. Outside the lattice it settles nothing: over `K = ℚ` for `L = sl₂`, take `ν(aω) = a/2` (so `ν(ω) = ½` and `ν(α) = 1`) and the Verma module of highest weight `λ = −ω/2`, which is irreducible because its raising operator sends `F^k v` to `k(λ(H) − k + 1) F^(k−1) v = −k(k − ½) F^(k−1) v ≠ 0` for `k ≥ 1`; its weights `λ − kα` have `ν = −¼ − k ∉ ½ℤ`, matching `ν(λ) = −¼`, so both sides fail.
- For the torus `G = G_m`, which is reductive and not semisimple, there are no roots; the irreducible algebraic representations are the characters `z ↦ z^k`, each with the single weight `k`, and both sides read `ν(k) ∈ ½ℤ`.

*Needs:* §5.1; Mathlib `LieAlgebra.IsKilling.rootSystem`, `RootPairing.Base`, `LieModule.genWeightSpace`, `LieModule.IsIrreducible`; Tau Ceti `TauCeti.IsHighestWeightVector`, `TauCeti.IsHighestWeightVector.genWeightSpace_ne_bot`, `TauCeti.IsHighestWeightVector.isDominantIntegral`, `TauCeti.posRootCone`, `TauCeti.sub_mem_posRootCone_of_genWeightSpace_ne_bot_of_isHighestWeightVector_of_lieSpan_eq_top`, `TauCeti.lieSpan_singleton_eq_top_of_ne_zero`, `TauCeti.nonempty_lieModuleEquiv_of_isHighestWeightVector` (finite-dimensional `M` as `L(λ)`); ReductiveGroups §10B (the group form).

### 5.3 Weight patterns

Fix `q > 1`. Define `weightPattern q A i`, for a complex endomorphism `A` of a finite-dimensional space `V` and `i ∈ ℤ`, as the dimension of the sum of the eigenspaces of `A` at the eigenvalues of absolute value `q^(i/2)`; for a semisimple `A` all of whose eigenvalues have absolute value in `q^(ℤ/2)` this is `n_A(i)`, the sum of the multiplicities of the eigenvalues of absolute value `q^(i/2)`. Define `IsGeometricPoint q s`, for a point `s` of a finite-type commutative Hopf `ℂ`-algebra `H` (an element of `G(ℂ)`), as the condition that on every finitely generated `H`-comodule every eigenvalue of the action of `s` has absolute value `q^(i/2)` for some `i ∈ ℤ`. For a `q`-geometric semisimple `s` in a connected complex reductive group `G`, its weight pattern is the function `(τ, i) ↦ n_(τ(s))(i)` on pairs of a finite-dimensional algebraic representation `τ` and an integer `i` (CH §10.1, p.32, CH line 1550).

Prove the following about `weightPattern` and `IsGeometricPoint`.

- `n_A(i)` is the sum of the multiplicities of the eigenvalues of absolute value `q^(i/2)`; since `i ↦ q^(i/2)` is injective, each eigenvalue is counted for exactly one `i`, and `Σ_i n_A(i) = dim V`.
- Patterns add under direct sums; the tensor unit has `n(0) = 1` and `n(i) = 0` otherwise.
- `n_(A⊗B)(i) = Σ_(j+k=i) n_A(j) n_B(k)`; `n_(A∨)(i) = n_A(−i)` for the contragredient.
- Similar operators, and conjugate group elements, have equal patterns; isomorphic representations give equal patterns.
- If `s` is `q`-geometric in one faithful representation it is `q`-geometric in every representation, because every finite-dimensional algebraic representation of an affine algebraic group is a subquotient of a finite direct sum of tensor products of a faithful one and its dual (ReductiveGroups Layer 1), and the eigenvalues of `s` there are products of eigenvalues on the faithful representation and their inverses.

**Checks.**

- The zero space gives the zero function; the one-dimensional trivial representation gives the indicator of `0`.
- In `SL₂`, `diag(q^(1/2), q^(−1/2))` has pattern `1` at `±1` in the standard representation, and its tensor square has `1, 2, 1` at `2, 0, −2`.
- At a real point the pattern records the exponent, not `q`: for the real cocharacter `ν = (½, −½)` of `SL₂`, the point `q^ν` is `diag(2, 1/2)` at `q = 4` and `diag(4, 1/4)` at `q = 16`; in the standard representation both have pattern `1` at `±1`, since `2 = 4^(1/2)` and `4 = 16^(1/2)`, while their traces `Σ_i n(i) q^(i/2)` are `5/2` and `17/4`. Normalizing by `q^i` instead of `q^(i/2)` would put these eigenvalues in no integer degree.
- In `SL₂`, `diag(i, −i)` and the identity have the same pattern in every algebraic representation (all absolute values are `1`) but are not conjugate; the pattern forgets phases.
- For a non-semisimple `A` the eigenspace definition undercounts: a Jordan block of size `2` at the eigenvalue `q^(1/2)` has `weightPattern q A 1 = 1`, not `2`; the pattern is only used at semisimple points, where eigenspaces and generalized eigenspaces agree.
- A `q`-geometric point is one in the sense of the Conventions: `diag(2, 1/2)` in `SL₂` is `q`-geometric for `q = 4` and not for `q = 3`, and for `q = 3` the points `diag(2, 1/2)` and `diag(5, 1/5)` have equal patterns (every pattern value is zero except the multiplicity of the eigenvalue `1`) although their positive real parts are not conjugate; the geometric hypothesis of §5.5 is not removable.

*Needs:* ReductiveGroups Layers 1, 4.

### 5.4 Positive real part

For a complex torus `T` with character lattice `X`, every `s ∈ T(ℂ) = Hom(X, ℂ×)` factors uniquely as `s = r(s)u(s)` with `χ(r(s)) = |χ(s)| ∈ ℝ_(>0)` and `|χ(u(s))| = 1` for every `χ ∈ X`. For a semisimple `s` in a connected complex reductive group `G`, choose a maximal torus containing `s` and define the positive real part `r(s)` there; its `G(ℂ)`-conjugacy class does not depend on the choice, and after moving `s` into a fixed maximal torus `r(s)` is well defined up to the Weyl group. (CH §6, p.16, CH line 781, gives the polar decomposition `T(ℂ) = T_ℝ · T_c`, `T_ℝ = Hom(X, ℝ_(>0))`, `T_c = Hom(X, S¹)`, of which `r(s)` is the `T_ℝ`-component and `u(s)` the compact one; CH §8.1, p.28, CH line 1334, writes that component as `q^(ℜ(s))` with `ℜ(s)` in the real Lie algebra `Lie(T_ℝ)`, so `r(s) = q^(ℜ(s))`; CH §10.1, proof of Proposition 10.2, p.32, CH line 1555, uses `ℜ(s)`.)

Define `IsPositiveRealPart s r`, for points `s, r` of `H`, as the condition that on every finitely generated `H`-comodule `r` acts on the `λ`-eigenspace of `s` by the scalar `|λ|`, for every `λ ∈ ℂ`. For semisimple `s` the element `r(s)` satisfies it, and it determines `r`: a finite-type `H` has a faithful finitely generated comodule `V` (ReductiveGroups Layer 1), on `V` the eigenspaces of a semisimple `s` span, so the action of `r` on `V` is fixed, and a point is determined by its action on a faithful comodule (Tau Ceti `TauCeti.Comodule.pointsAction_injective_of_isFaithful`). Here the matrix coefficients of `V` generate `H` only together with their antipode images (`TauCeti.Comodule.isFaithful_iff_matrixCoefficientSubalgebra_sup_antipode_eq_top`; ReductiveGroups Layer 1), and a point sends the antipode image of a coefficient to the corresponding entry of the inverse matrix, which its action on `V` also fixes.

A proof can proceed as follows. Absolute value is multiplicative, so `χ ↦ |χ(s)|` is a homomorphism `X → ℝ_(>0) ⊂ ℂ×`, a point of `T(ℂ)`; no logarithm or basis of `X` is chosen. Two maximal tori containing `s` are maximal tori of the connected group `Z_G(s)°` and are conjugate by an element fixing `s`, which transports `r(s)`. The Weyl group commutes with `χ ↦ |χ(·)|`. In an algebraic representation the eigenvalues of `τ(r(s))` are the absolute values of those of `τ(s)`, with the same multiplicities.

Prove the following about `r(s)` and `IsPositiveRealPart`.

- `χ(r(s)) = |χ(s)|` for every `χ ∈ X`, and this determines `r(s)`.
- `r(st) = r(s)r(t)` and `r(1) = 1` inside a fixed torus.
- `r(w·s) = w·r(s)` for `w` in the Weyl group.
- The multiset of eigenvalues of `τ(r(s))` is the multiset of absolute values of the eigenvalues of `τ(s)`; hence `tr τ(r(s)) = Σ_i n_(τ(s))(i) q^(i/2)` for `q`-geometric `s`.
- `IsPositiveRealPart s (r(s))` holds for semisimple `s`, and `IsPositiveRealPart s r` for semisimple `s` forces `r = r(s)`.

**Checks.**

- If every character value has absolute value `1`, then `r(s) = 1`; compact phases are discarded.
- In `SL₂`, `r(diag(z, z⁻¹)) = diag(|z|, |z|⁻¹)`; the Weyl involution replaces `|z|` by `|z|⁻¹`, and both represent one conjugacy class.
- For `z = i`, `r(s)` is the identity although `s = diag(i, −i)` is not.
- For `PGL₂` the character lattice is `ℤα`; `r` is computed from `|α(s)|` only, and `diag(z, 1)` and `diag(−z, 1)` have the same positive part.
- For the unipotent `s = [[1, 1], [0, 1]]` in `SL₂` both `s` itself and `1` satisfy `IsPositiveRealPart s r`: the `1`-eigenspace of `s` on any representation is its space of fixed vectors, on which both act as the identity, and `s` has no other eigenvalue. The predicate pins a positive real part only at semisimple points, which is why §5.5 assumes semisimplicity.
- For `G = G_m`, `H = ℂ[t, t⁻¹]`, the character `z ↦ z` is a faithful comodule whose only matrix coefficient `t` generates the subalgebra `ℂ[t]`, which misses `t⁻¹`; with its antipode image `t⁻¹` it generates `H`. For `s = 2i` the predicate makes `r` act by `|2i| = 2` on this character, so `r(t) = 2`; then `r(t⁻¹) = 1/2` is forced, matching the dual character, on which `s` acts by `(2i)⁻¹`, of absolute value `1/2`. So `r` is the point `2`, and a uniqueness argument that only evaluated `r` on the coefficient subalgebra `ℂ[t]` would not reach all of `H`.

*Needs:* ReductiveGroups Layers 1, 4, 7; Tau Ceti `TauCeti.Comodule.pointsAction_injective_of_isFaithful`, `TauCeti.Comodule.isFaithful_iff_matrixCoefficientSubalgebra_sup_antipode_eq_top`.

### 5.5 Recognition of the positive real part

Fix `q > 1`. If semisimple `s, s'` in a connected complex reductive group `G` are `q`-geometric and have equal weight patterns, then `r(s)` and `r(s')` are `G(ℂ)`-conjugate, equivalently Weyl-conjugate after moving them into a fixed maximal torus. It suffices to compare patterns on irreducible representations. No conjugacy of `s` and `s'` themselves is asserted. Prove `exists_conj_positiveRealPart_of_weightPattern_eq`, the statement on the Hopf-algebra carrier of the Conventions: for `H` with `TauCeti.reductiveCommHopfAlgProperty`, semisimple points `s, s'` (`TauCeti.HopfAlgebra.IsSemisimplePoint`) with `IsGeometricPoint q s`, `IsGeometricPoint q s'` and equal values of `weightPattern q` on every finitely generated comodule in every degree, and points `r, r'` with `IsPositiveRealPart s r` and `IsPositiveRealPart s' r'`, some point `g` satisfies `g r g⁻¹ = r'` in the convolution group of points. Existence of `r(s)` is §5.4; the Lean statement takes `r, r'` as data satisfying the predicate, which by §5.4 forces `r = r(s)` and `r' = r(s')`. (CH §10.1, Proposition 10.2, p.32, CH line 1554.)

A proof can proceed as follows. For each `τ`, `tr τ(r(s)) = Σ_i n_(τ(s))(i) q^(i/2)` by §5.4, so equal patterns give equal traces at `r(s)` and `r(s')` in every algebraic representation; direct-sum additivity reduces to irreducibles. Both are semisimple, so ReductiveGroupsPartIII §0.5 (characters separate semisimple classes in a connected reductive group over an algebraically closed field) gives conjugacy. Nothing in the argument uses a finite centre: a central torus is allowed.

**Checks.**

- `diag(i, −i)` and `1` in `SL₂` have equal patterns; the conclusion `r = 1` for both holds, and the elements are not conjugate.
- `diag(q^(1/2), q^(−1/2))` and `diag(q^(−1/2), q^(1/2))` have equal patterns and conjugate positive parts (Weyl involution).
- `diag(q^(1/2), q^(−1/2))` and the identity have different patterns in the standard representation (`1` at `±1` versus `2` at `0`), and their positive parts are not conjugate.
- The semisimplicity hypotheses are not removable: in `SL₂` take `s = s' = u = [[1, 1], [0, 1]]`, which is `q`-geometric (its only eigenvalue is `1`) and has equal patterns with itself, and `r = u`, `r' = 1`; both satisfy the positive-real-part predicate for `u` (§5.4), but `u` and `1` are not conjugate. The pair `u` and `1` itself would not do: on the standard representation `weightPattern q u 0 = dim ker(u − 1) = 1`, while `weightPattern q 1 0 = 2`.
- The group need not be semisimple: for `G = G_m`, `q = 4`, `s = 2i` and `s' = 2`, the character `z ↦ z^k` gives eigenvalues `(2i)^k` and `2^k`, both of absolute value `4^(k/2)`, so both patterns are `1` at degree `k`; the positive parts are both `2`, conjugate by `g = 1`, with trace `2^k` in that character, while `s` and `s'` are distinct and so not conjugate in the commutative `G_m`. The points `2` and `1/2` have patterns `1` at `k` and at `−k`, which differ, and indeed `2` and `1/2` are not conjugate in `G_m`.
- For the trivial group `H = ℂ` the only point is `1`, every hypothesis holds and `g = 1` conjugates.

## Downstream consumers

The recognition theorems of Layers 1–4 and the positive-real-part recognition of Layer 5 are the representation-theoretic inputs of the Ciubotaru–Harris argument for the generalized Ramanujan and Arthur conjectures over function fields: Lemma 2.4 compares Frobenius weight filtrations of Langlands parameters, and Proposition 10.2 identifies the real part of a Satake parameter from its weight patterns. Any consumer comparing representation-indexed filtrations or weight data of parameters for a complex reductive dual group uses these interfaces; none of them feeds back into this roadmap.

GlobalShtukasAndFunctionFieldLanglandsPartII §8.0 is such a consumer. For `ν` in the real cocharacter space of the complex dual torus and an algebraic representation `τ`, its multiplicity function `n_(ν,τ)(i)`, the number of weights `μ` of `τ`, counted with multiplicity, with `2⟨μ, ν⟩ = i`, is `weightPattern q (τ(q^ν)) i` for every `q > 1`, because `τ(q^ν)` acts on the `μ`-weight space of `τ` by the positive real `q^⟨μ,ν⟩`, of absolute value `q^(i/2)` exactly when `2⟨μ, ν⟩ = i`; for the adjoint representation of `SL₃` and `ν = 0` both sides are `8` at `i = 0`, although that representation has only `7` distinct weights (the six roots, and `0` with multiplicity two). The geometric `ν` of that section are those for which `q^ν` satisfies `IsGeometricPoint q`, and `q^ν` is its own positive real part. So §5.5 applied to `q^ν` and `q^ν′` makes them `G(ℂ)`-conjugate, hence Weyl-conjugate as points of the torus, hence `ν` and `ν′` Weyl-conjugate, since `ν ↦ q^ν` is injective and Weyl-equivariant on the real cocharacter space. That section adds the normalization across residue cardinalities, the relative Weyl group refinement and its Aubert-involution identity, the three items that Scope and ownership leaves to it. SmoothRepresentationsOfLocalGroupsPartII consumes the positive real part of §5.4 and the half-integrality test of §5.2 for real parts of spherical Satake parameters.

## Worked examples

- **`sl(3)`.** The zero, minimal and regular triples have neutral elements `0`, `diag(1, 0, −1)`, `diag(2, 0, −2)`; their standard filtrations have jumps `(3 at 0)`, `(1 at −1, 1 at 0, 1 at 1)`, `(1 at −2, 1 at 0, 1 at 2)`, and their adjoint graded multiplicities are `(8 at 0)`, `(1, 2, 2, 2, 1)` on `[−2, 2]`, and `(1, 0, 2, 0, 2, 0, 2, 0, 1)` on `[−4, 4]`.
- **`so(8)`.** The partition `(4, 4)` gives two classes, equal on `K⁸` and on the adjoint module, separated by `L(ω₄)` (top indices `4`, `3`).
- **Exceptional candidates.** Root counts `12, 48, 72, 126, 240`; candidate counts `7, 28, 34, 274, 245`, all with distinct profiles; principal top degrees `10, 22, 22, 34, 58`; total dimensions `14, 52, 78, 133, 248`.
- **Weight patterns.** In `SL₂`, the parameters `diag(q^(1/2)e^(iθ), q^(−1/2)e^(−iθ))` share one pattern and one positive part for all `θ`.
