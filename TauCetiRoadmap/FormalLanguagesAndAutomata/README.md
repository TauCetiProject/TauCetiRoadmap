# Roadmap: formal languages and automata

**Summary.** A language is a set of finite words over an alphabet. Two classical families of
languages are studied here: the regular languages, those accepted by a deterministic automaton
with finitely many states, and the context-free languages, those generated by a grammar with
finitely many rules each rewriting one nonterminal. This roadmap proves the main structure theorems
for both families. Every target is a statement about languages, automata or grammars; no
procedure for converting, minimizing or parsing is specified. The alphabet is arbitrary, and it is
assumed finite only in the two statements that need it.

The headline theorems are these.

- *Kleene's theorem.* The language matched by a regular expression is regular, over any alphabet.
  Conversely, over a finite alphabet every regular language is matched by some regular expression;
  over an infinite alphabet the converse fails, since a regular expression mentions only finitely
  many letters.
- *Uniqueness of the minimal automaton.* A finite deterministic automaton has the least number of
  states among all automata for its language exactly when every state is reachable from the start
  state and no two states accept the same language from there. Every regular language has such an
  automaton, its number of states is the number of left quotients of the language, and any two
  minimal automata for the same language differ only by a renaming of their states.
- *The pumping lemma for context-free languages.* For every context-free language there is a
  positive integer `p` such that every word of the language of length at least `p` splits as
  `u v x y z` with `v y` nonempty and `v x y` of length at most `p`, and every word `u vⁱ x yⁱ z`
  is in the language. In consequence the language of the words `0ⁿ 1ⁿ 2ⁿ` is not context-free.
- *Intersection with a regular language.* The intersection of a context-free language and a
  regular language is context-free, over any alphabet. The intersection of two context-free
  languages need not be, and neither need the complement of one.

The roadmap builds on Mathlib's automata library, which supplies the automata, regular
expressions and grammars, the Myhill–Nerode characterization of regular languages by finitely
many left quotients with the automaton of left quotients, the pumping lemma for regular languages,
closure of regular languages under complement, union, intersection and reversal, and closure of
context-free languages under reversal. Tau Ceti's automata with output, which
ClassicalArithmeticCompletion uses for automatic sequences, are a different object and are not
used.

Layer 1 below proves closure of regular languages under concatenation and star and both halves
of Kleene's theorem. Layer 2 treats accessible, reduced and minimal automata and their
uniqueness. Layer 3 proves the Chomsky normal form, the pumping lemma and the example `0ⁿ 1ⁿ 2ⁿ`.
Layer 4, which depends on Layer 3, proves the closure properties of context-free languages and
their failures; Layers 1, 2 and 3 depend on no other layer.

## Conventions

- **Words and languages.** A word over `α` is a `List α`; a language is Mathlib's
  `Language α`, which is `Set (List α)`. Union is `L₁ + L₂`, intersection `L₁ ⊓ L₂`, concatenation
  `L₁ * L₂` (all `x ++ y` with `x ∈ L₁`, `y ∈ L₂`), Kleene star `L∗` (all concatenations of finitely
  many words of `L`, the empty concatenation included), complement `Lᶜ`, and `1` is the language
  `{[]}` whose only word is the empty word, so `L - 1` is `L` with the empty word removed. The
  alphabet `α` is a type of any size and any universe; a hypothesis `[Finite α]` is stated where it
  is used.
- **Automata.** A deterministic automaton is Mathlib's `DFA α σ`: a transition function
  `step : σ → α → σ`, a start state and a set of accepting states. The structure does not ask `σ`
  to be finite. `M.evalFrom s x` is the state reached from `s` after reading `x` from its first
  letter on, `M.eval x` the state reached from the start state, `M.acceptsFrom s` the set of words
  leading from `s` to an accepting state, and `M.accepts` the language of `M`. A language is
  regular, `Language.IsRegular`, when it is the language of an automaton whose state type is a
  `Fintype` in `Type`; by `Language.isRegular_iff` the universe of the state type does not matter.
  An isomorphism of automata is a bijection `e : σ ≃ τ` of states with `DFA.reindex e M = N`,
  which says that `e` carries the start state, the transitions and the accepting states of `M` to
  those of `N`.
- **Left quotients.** The left quotient of `L` by a word `x` is
  `L.leftQuotient x = {y | x ++ y ∈ L}`. Mathlib's `Language.toDFA L` is the automaton whose states
  are the left quotients of `L`, with start state `L`, transition from `K` on the letter `a` to
  `K.leftQuotient [a]`, and accepting states those containing the empty word; its language is `L`.
  For an automaton `M`, `M.accepts.leftQuotient x = M.acceptsFrom (M.eval x)`.
- **Regular expressions.** Mathlib's `RegularExpression α` has the constructors `0`, `1`,
  `char a`, `P + Q`, `P * Q` and `star P`, and `P.matches'` is its language: the empty language,
  `{[]}`, `{[a]}`, union, concatenation and Kleene star.
- **Grammars.** A context-free grammar is Mathlib's `ContextFreeGrammar T`: a type `NT : Type` of
  nonterminals, an initial nonterminal and a `Finset` of rules, each rewriting one nonterminal to a
  list of `Symbol T NT`. Rules with an empty right-hand side and rules rewriting one nonterminal to
  another are allowed. `g.language` is the set of words of terminals derivable from the initial
  nonterminal, and a language is context-free, `Language.IsContextFree`, when it is `g.language`
  for some grammar `g`. Since the rules are finitely many, only finitely many terminals occur in
  them, and every word of `g.language` is a word over those terminals.
- **Repetition.** `vⁱ` is the word `(List.replicate i v).flatten`, the concatenation of `i` copies
  of `v`; `v⁰` is the empty word.

## Layer 1: Kleene's theorem

### 1.1 Concatenation and Kleene star

- `isRegular_mul`: if `L₁` and `L₂` are regular, so is `L₁ * L₂`.
- `isRegular_kstar`: if `L` is regular, so is `L∗`.

Both hold over any alphabet (Watrous, Theorem 4.2, p. 32, for finite alphabets; the constructions
do not use the finiteness of the alphabet). Union, intersection, complement and reversal are
already in the library (`Language.IsRegular.add`, `Language.IsRegular.inf`,
`Language.IsRegular.compl`, `Language.IsRegular.reverse`).

Route: for the concatenation of the languages of finite automata `M₁` on `σ₁` and `M₂` on `σ₂`,
take the ε-automaton on `σ₁ ⊕ σ₂` that runs `M₁` on the left summand and `M₂` on the right one,
starts at the start of `M₁`, accepts at the accepting states of `M₂`, and has an ε-move from each
accepting state of `M₁` to the start of `M₂`. For the star of the language of `M` on `σ`, take the
states `Option σ`: the extra state `none` is the start and the only accepting state, and ε-moves
lead from `none` to the start of `M` and from each accepting state of `M` to `none`. Pass to an
automaton without ε-moves by `εNFA.toNFA_correct` and to a deterministic one on the finite type of
subsets by `NFA.toDFA_correct`, and conclude with `Language.isRegular_iff`, which allows a finite
state type in any universe. In the star construction the only moves into `none` are the ε-moves
from the accepting states of `M`, so making `none` accepting adds only the empty word (see the
second Check).

**Checks.**
- Over the alphabet `{a, b}`, the language of words with an even number of `b` is regular (two
  states), and its concatenation with `{[a]}` is the language of words ending in `a` whose number
  of `b` is even; it has three left quotients, so its automata need three states.
- The new state is needed. Let `L = {aⁿ b : n ≥ 0}`, accepted by an automaton whose start state
  loops on `a` and moves on `b` to an accepting state. Making that start state accepting and adding
  an ε-move back to it from the accepting state accepts the word `a`, which is not in `L∗`, since
  every nonempty word of `L∗` ends in `b`.
- Over the alphabet `ℕ`, the language `{[0]}` is regular (start, accepting, dead), and its star,
  the words `0ⁿ`, has two left quotients and is accepted by two states: an accepting state that
  loops on `0` and a dead state reached on every other letter. The infinitely many letters only
  enlarge the set of letters leading to the dead state.

*Needs:* Mathlib `DFA.toNFA_correct`, `NFA.toεNFA_correct`, `εNFA.toNFA_correct`,
`NFA.toDFA_correct`, `Language.isRegular_iff`, `Language.mem_mul`, `Language.mem_kstar`.

### 1.2 From regular expressions to automata

Prove `isRegular_matches'`: for every regular expression `P` over any alphabet, `P.matches'` is
regular (Kleene, 1956; Watrous, Proposition 4.8, p. 41, for finite alphabets).

Route: induction on `P`. The empty language and `{[]}` have two-state automata, `{[a]}` a
three-state one (start, accept, dead), whose transition on every letter other than `a` goes to the
dead state, so the alphabet may be infinite. Union is `Language.IsRegular.add`; concatenation and
star are 1.1.

**Checks.**
- Over `{a, b}`, the expressions `star (char a + char b)` and
  `star (char a) * star (char b + char a)` match the same language, all words, and the automaton of left quotients of that language has one
  state.
- On 300 random regular expressions over `{a, b}` of depth at most 4, the automaton obtained by the
  constructions above accepts exactly the matched words of length at most 7.

*Needs:* 1.1; Mathlib `RegularExpression.matches'`, `Language.IsRegular.add`.

### 1.3 From automata to regular expressions

Prove `exists_matches'_eq_of_isRegular`: if the alphabet is finite and `L` is regular, then
`L = P.matches'` for some regular expression `P` (Kleene, 1956; McNaughton and Yamada, 1960;
Watrous, Theorem 4.9, p. 42; Gallier–Quaintance, Proposition 5.6, p. 140).

Route: number the states of an automaton for `L` as `1, …, n`, and let `R(i, j, k)` be the set of
words leading from `i` to `j` through intermediate states numbered at most `k`. Each `R(i, j, 0)`
is a finite set of one-letter words, together with the empty word when `i = j`, and so is matched
by a regular expression because the alphabet is finite; the recursion
`R(i, j, k) = R(i, j, k − 1) + R(i, k, k − 1) * R(k, k, k − 1)∗ * R(k, j, k − 1)` keeps this
property, and `L` is the finite union of `R(start, j, n)` over the accepting `j`. A route through
Arden's rule, Mathlib's `Language.self_eq_mul_add_iff`, which solves `X = M * X + N` by
`X = M∗ * N` when `M` does not contain the empty word, works as well.

**Checks.**
- The finiteness of the alphabet is needed. Over `ℕ` the language of all one-letter words is
  regular (three states: start, after one letter, dead), but the words matched by a regular
  expression use only the finitely many letters it mentions, so no expression matches it.
- On every automaton with at most six states obtained in the Check of 1.2, the expression of the
  recursion above matches the same words of length at most 7 as the automaton.
- An automaton with no accepting state gives the empty union, matched by `0`.

*Needs:* Mathlib `DFA.accepts`, `DFA.eval`, `RegularExpression.matches'`,
`Language.self_eq_mul_add_iff` (for the second route), `Fintype.equivFin`.

### Examples

The languages over `{a, b}` of words with an even number of `b`, of words ending in `a`, and of
all words, with automata and expressions for each; the language of one-letter words over `ℕ`.

### Dependencies

Mathlib `DFA.toNFA_correct`, `NFA.toεNFA_correct`, `εNFA.toNFA_correct`, `NFA.toDFA_correct`,
`Language.isRegular_iff`, `Language.mem_mul`, `Language.mem_kstar`,
`Language.IsRegular.add`, `RegularExpression.matches'`, `Language.self_eq_mul_add_iff`,
`DFA.accepts`, `DFA.eval`, `Fintype.equivFin`. No other layer.

## Layer 2: minimal automata

### 2.1 Accessible and reduced automata

Define `IsAccessible` and `IsReduced` for an automaton `M : DFA α σ` with any state type:

- `IsAccessible M`: every state is `M.eval x` for some word `x`;
- `IsReduced M`: the map `s ↦ M.acceptsFrom s` is injective, so distinct states accept distinct
  languages.

Prove `exists_reindex_eq_toDFA`: if `M` is accessible and reduced, some bijection `e` from the
states of `M` to the left quotients of `M.accepts` has `DFA.reindex e M = M.accepts.toDFA`; so `M`
is isomorphic to the automaton of left quotients of its language. No finiteness is assumed
(Gallier–Quaintance, Theorem 6.13, p. 169, which identifies the automaton of left quotients with
the quotient of a trim automaton by state equivalence; an automaton that is reduced in the sense
above is its own quotient, while Gallier–Quaintance use "reduced" for what is called accessible
here).

Route: put `e s = M.acceptsFrom s`. It lands among the left quotients because accessibility gives
`s = M.eval x` and `M.acceptsFrom (M.eval x) = M.accepts.leftQuotient x`
(`Language.leftQuotient_accepts_apply`); it is onto for the same reason and one to one because `M`
is reduced. It carries the start state to `M.accepts`, the transition on `a` to the left quotient
by `[a]` (`Language.step_toDFA`, `Language.leftQuotient_append`), and a state is accepting
exactly when its language contains the empty word (`Language.mem_accept_toDFA`).

**Checks.**
- The automaton of left quotients of any language is accessible and reduced: its state `K` is
  reached by any `x` with `K = L.leftQuotient x`, and it accepts the language `K` from `K`.
- Over `{a}`, the automaton with states `0, 1`, both accepting, transitions `0 → 1 → 0` and start
  `0` is accessible but not reduced: both states accept every word. Its language `a∗` has one left
  quotient.
- The two-state automaton for the words with an even number of `a`, with a third state added that
  is unreachable and accepts nothing, is reduced (the three state languages are the even words,
  the odd words and the empty language) but not accessible.

*Needs:* Mathlib `DFA.reindex`, `DFA.acceptsFrom`, `Language.toDFA`, `Language.start_toDFA`,
`Language.step_toDFA`, `Language.mem_accept_toDFA`, `Language.leftQuotient_accepts_apply`,
`Language.leftQuotient_append`. (Proved here, following the cited route.)

### 2.2 Minimal automata and the number of left quotients

Define `IsMinimal`: for `M : DFA α σ` with `[Fintype σ]`, `IsMinimal M` says that every
automaton on `Fin n` with the language of `M` has `Fintype.card σ ≤ n`. Comparing with
automata on `Fin n` loses nothing, since every automaton with finitely many states is a reindexing
of one on some `Fin n` (`Fintype.equivFin`), with the same language (`DFA.accepts_reindex`).

- For every automaton `M` with finitely many states, the number of left quotients of `M.accepts`
  is at most `Fintype.card σ`, since every left quotient is `M.acceptsFrom s` for a reachable state
  `s` (Gallier–Quaintance, Proposition 6.9, p. 165).
- `exists_isMinimal`: every regular language is the language of a minimal automaton with state
  type in `Type`.
- `isMinimal_iff`: an automaton with finitely many states is minimal if and only if it is
  accessible and reduced.
- `IsMinimal.card_eq`: a minimal automaton has exactly as many states as its language has left
  quotients (Gallier–Quaintance, Proposition 6.10, p. 165, and Theorem 6.14, p. 171).

Route: the automaton of left quotients, which has finitely many states by
`Language.IsRegular.finite_range_leftQuotient`, reindexed to `Fin n`, attains the lower bound of
the first bullet, so it is minimal; this gives `exists_isMinimal`. An automaton that is not
accessible loses states, and keeps its language, when restricted to its reachable states; one that
is not reduced has more states than left quotients of its reachable states. Conversely an
accessible and reduced automaton is in bijection with the left quotients by 2.1, and the first
bullet compares it with every other automaton.

**Checks.**
- For `k = 1, 2, 3, 4`, the language over `{a, b}` of words whose `k`-th letter from the end is
  `a` has `2ᵏ` left quotients, one for each word of length `k` recording the last `k` letters read,
  so each of its minimal automata has `2ᵏ` states.
- Over the empty alphabet the only word is the empty word, every accessible automaton has one
  state, and the minimal automata are exactly the one-state automata.
- Among all automata over `{a, b}` with start state `0` and at most three states (1054 languages
  occur), an automaton has the least number of states for its language exactly when it is
  accessible and reduced, and that number is the number of left quotients; the same holds over
  one letter (18 languages) and over the empty alphabet (2 languages).
- The minimality of `M` is not decided by `M` alone being accessible: the accessible automaton
  of the second Check of 2.1 has two states for a language with one left quotient.

*Needs:* 2.1; Mathlib `Language.leftQuotient_accepts_apply`, `Language.toDFA`,
`Language.accepts_toDFA`, `Language.IsRegular.finite_range_leftQuotient`, `DFA.reindex`,
`DFA.accepts_reindex`, `Fintype.equivFin`. (Proved here, except as cited.)

### 2.3 Uniqueness of the minimal automaton

Prove `IsMinimal.exists_reindex_eq`: if `M` on `σ` and `N` on `τ`, with finitely many states, are
minimal and accept the same language, some bijection `e : σ ≃ τ` has `DFA.reindex e M = N`
(Myhill, 1957, and Nerode, 1958; Gallier–Quaintance, Proposition 6.10, p. 165, and Theorem 6.13,
p. 169, from which this is derived).

Route: by 2.2 both automata are accessible and reduced, so by 2.1 each is isomorphic to the
automaton of left quotients of their common language; compose the first bijection with the
inverse of the second.

**Checks.**
- Among the automata with at most three states over `{a, b}`, any two minimal automata with the
  same language are related by a bijection of states fixing the start state and preserving
  transitions and accepting states (1028 pairs).
- Without minimality the statement fails: the one-state automaton accepting every word over `{a}`
  and the accessible two-state automaton of the second Check of 2.1 accept the same language and
  have different numbers of states.

*Needs:* 2.1, 2.2; Mathlib `DFA.reindex`. (Proved here, following the cited route.)

### Examples

The automata of the Checks: the languages fixing the `k`-th letter from the end, the two
non-minimal automata of 2.1, and the automata over the empty alphabet.

### Dependencies

Mathlib `DFA.reindex`, `DFA.accepts_reindex`, `DFA.acceptsFrom`, `Language.toDFA`,
`Language.start_toDFA`, `Language.step_toDFA`, `Language.mem_accept_toDFA`,
`Language.accepts_toDFA`, `Language.leftQuotient_accepts_apply`, `Language.leftQuotient_append`,
`Language.IsRegular.finite_range_leftQuotient`, `Fintype.equivFin`. No other layer.

## Layer 3: Chomsky normal form and the pumping lemma

### 3.1 Chomsky normal form

Define `IsChomskyNormalForm`: a grammar `g` satisfies `IsChomskyNormalForm g` when the
right-hand side of every rule is a single terminal or two nonterminals.

Prove `exists_isChomskyNormalForm`: for every context-free language `L`, some grammar in Chomsky
normal form generates `L - 1`, the language `L` with the empty word removed (Chomsky, 1959;
Watrous, Theorem 8.2, p. 83, and Gallier–Quaintance, Proposition 7.4, p. 218, whose normal forms
add a rule from a new initial nonterminal to the empty word when `L` contains it).

Route: add a new initial nonterminal; remove the rules with empty right-hand side, adding for each
rule every variant with some occurrences of nullable nonterminals deleted; remove the rules that
rewrite one nonterminal to another by composing them with the remaining rules; replace each
terminal in a right-hand side of length at least two by a new nonterminal with a single rule to
it; split right-hand sides of length at least three with new nonterminals. Each step keeps the
rules finitely many and the generated language, up to the empty word. Over an infinite alphabet
only the finitely many terminals occurring in the rules are involved.

**Checks.**
- A grammar in Chomsky normal form derives only nonempty words from each nonterminal, so the
  empty word must be removed: `{[]}` is context-free and `{[]} - 1` is empty, generated by the
  grammar with no rules.
- The nonempty balanced words of parentheses are generated by the rules `D → L R`, `D → L E`,
  `D → D D`, `E → D R`, `L → (`, `R → )`; the grammar generates 1, 2, 5, 14, 42 and 132 words of
  lengths 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12, the Catalan numbers, and no word of odd length.
- The grammar with the rules `S → a S b` and `S → a b` is not in Chomsky normal form, since its
  right-hand sides mix terminals and nonterminals or have two terminals; its language
  `{aⁿ bⁿ : n ≥ 1}` is generated by `S → A X`, `S → A B`, `X → S B`, `A → a`, `B → b`, which is.

*Needs:* Mathlib `ContextFreeGrammar`, `ContextFreeRule`, `ContextFreeGrammar.language`,
`ContextFreeGrammar.Derives`, `ContextFreeGrammar.Derives.append_left`,
`ContextFreeGrammar.Derives.append_right`, `Language.sub_def`. (Proved here, following the
cited route.)

### 3.2 The pumping lemma

Prove `exists_pumping`: for every context-free language `L` there is `p > 0` such that every
`w ∈ L` with `p ≤ |w|` is `u ++ v ++ x ++ y ++ z` with `v ++ y` nonempty, `|v ++ x ++ y| ≤ p`, and
`u ++ vⁱ ++ x ++ yⁱ ++ z ∈ L` for every `i ≥ 0` (Bar-Hillel, Perles and Shamir, 1961; Watrous,
Lemma 10.1, p. 97; Gallier–Quaintance, Theorem 7.15, p. 246, with every position marked, and
Proposition 7.16, p. 250).

Route (Watrous, proof of Lemma 10.1, pp. 98–101): take a grammar in Chomsky normal form for
`L - 1` (3.1) with `m` nonterminals occurring in its rules or as its initial nonterminal, and put
`p = 2ᵐ`; then every word of length at least `p` is nonempty. A derivation tree of a word of
length at least `2ᵐ` has depth more than `m`: a longest path from the root to a leaf has at
least `m + 1` nonterminal nodes, so some
nonterminal `X` repeats among the last `m + 1` of them; the subtrees at the two lowest occurrences
give `X ⇒* v X y`, `X ⇒* x` and the bound `|v x y| ≤ 2ᵐ`, and `v y` is nonempty because each
nonterminal node of a tree in Chomsky normal form has two nonterminal children or one terminal
child. Mathlib's derivations are sequences of rewriting steps; the trees are built from them by
induction on `ContextFreeGrammar.Derives`, or the argument is run on derivations directly.

**Checks.**
- For `{aⁿ bⁿ : n ≥ 0}` the constant `p = 2` works, with `v = a` and `y = b` taken at the middle of
  the word, and `p = 1` does not: for `a b` every split with `|v x y| ≤ 1` changes the count of
  only one letter.
- The condition that `v ++ y` is nonempty is what gives the statement content: with `v` and `y`
  allowed to be empty every word of every language could be split, and the argument of 3.3 would
  not go through.
- For the context-free language `L = {[]}` every `p > 0` works vacuously, while `p = 0` would
  require pumping the empty word with `v ++ y` nonempty and `|v x y| ≤ 0`, which is impossible.

*Needs:* 3.1; Mathlib `ContextFreeGrammar.Derives`, `ContextFreeGrammar.Produces`,
`ContextFreeRule.Rewrites`, `ContextFreeGrammar.Derives.append_left`,
`ContextFreeGrammar.Derives.append_right`. (Proved here, following the cited route.)

### 3.3 A language that is not context-free

Define `equalBlocks`, the language over `Fin 3` of the words `0ⁿ 1ⁿ 2ⁿ` for `n ≥ 0`, and prove
`not_isContextFree_equalBlocks`: it is not context-free (Bar-Hillel, Perles and Shamir, 1961;
Watrous, Proposition 10.2, p. 101; Gallier–Quaintance, Example 7.26, p. 251, for `n ≥ 1`).

Route: with `p` from 3.2, pump `0ᵖ 1ᵖ 2ᵖ`. Since `|v x y| ≤ p`, the word `v x y` meets at most two
of the three blocks, so `u v² x y² z` has more letters of one kind than of another unless it leaves
the block structure, and in both cases it is not in the language.

**Checks.**
- For `p = 1, …, 6` no split of `0ᵖ 1ᵖ 2ᵖ` with `v y` nonempty and `|v x y| ≤ p` keeps both
  `u x z` and `u v² x y² z` in the language.
- The two-block language `{0ⁿ 1ⁿ}` is context-free (`S → 0 S 1`, `S → ε`) and not regular: by
  Mathlib's pumping lemma for automata, `DFA.pumping_lemma`, pumping inside the first block of
  `0ᵖ 1ᵖ` unbalances the blocks. So over a finite alphabet with at least
  two letters, where every regular language is context-free (4.2), the regular languages are a
  proper part of the context-free ones. Two letters are needed: over a one-letter alphabet every
  context-free language is regular, by 3.2 (a language with pumping constant `p` is a finite set
  together with finitely many arithmetic progressions of difference `p!`), and over the empty
  alphabet the only languages are `∅` and `{[]}`. Over `ℕ`
  neither class contains the other: the language of all words is regular and not context-free
  (Check of 4.2), and `{0ⁿ 1ⁿ}` is context-free and not regular by the same argument.

*Needs:* 3.2; Mathlib `Language.IsContextFree`. (Proved here, following the cited route.)

### Examples

The balanced words of parentheses, `{aⁿ bⁿ}` with its grammars, and `{0ⁿ 1ⁿ 2ⁿ}`.

## Layer 4: closure properties

### 4.1 Union, concatenation and star

- `isContextFree_add`: the union of two context-free languages is context-free.
- `isContextFree_mul`: their concatenation is context-free.
- `isContextFree_kstar`: the Kleene star of a context-free language is context-free.

These hold over any alphabet (Watrous, Theorem 9.1, p. 87, for finite alphabets). Closure under
reversal is already in the library (`Language.IsContextFree.reverse`).

Route: with grammars `g₁` and `g₂`, take the nonterminals `Option (g₁.NT ⊕ g₂.NT)` with the new
initial nonterminal `none` and the rules of both grammars on their copies; add `none → S₁` and
`none → S₂` for the union, `none → S₁ S₂` for the concatenation, and for the star of `g` the rules
`none → ε` and `none → S none`. A derivation from `none` splits into derivations from the initial
nonterminals, since the two copies share no nonterminal.

**Checks.**
- The star rules `none → S none` and `none → ε` give exactly the concatenations of words of `L`.
  The variant with the rules `none → S S` and `none → S` in their place fails: for `L = {a}` it
  generates only `a` and `a a`, while `L∗` contains the empty word and `a a a`.
- `{0ⁿ 1ⁿ 2ᵐ}` is the concatenation of `{0ⁿ 1ⁿ}` and `{2ᵐ}`, and `{0ⁿ 1ᵐ 2ᵐ}` that of `{0ⁿ}` and
  `{1ᵐ 2ᵐ}`; both are context-free.

*Needs:* Mathlib `ContextFreeGrammar`, `ContextFreeGrammar.language`, `ContextFreeGrammar.Derives`,
`Language.mem_mul`, `Language.mem_kstar`.

### 4.2 Regular languages are context-free

Prove `isContextFree_of_isRegular`: over a finite alphabet every regular language is context-free
(Watrous, Theorem 9.2, p. 90; Gallier–Quaintance, Proposition 7.6, p. 222).

Route (as in Gallier–Quaintance, proof of Proposition 7.6): let the nonterminals be the states of
an automaton for `L`, with its start state as the initial nonterminal. Each transition, from `p` on
the letter `a` to `q`, becomes the rule `p → a q`, and each accepting state `p` gets the rule
`p → ε`. Then a state `p` derives the sentential form `x q` exactly when reading `x` from `p` ends
in `q`, which is proved by induction on `x`. There are finitely many rules since there are finitely
many states and letters.

**Checks.**
- The finiteness of the alphabet is needed: over `ℕ` the language of all words is regular, with one
  state, but the words of a context-free language use only the finitely many terminals of its
  rules, so it is not context-free.
- Over `{a, b}`, the automaton for an even number of `b` gives the four rules `p → a p`,
  `p → b q`, `q → a q`, `q → b p` and the rule `p → ε`.

*Needs:* Mathlib `DFA.evalFrom`, `DFA.accepts`, `ContextFreeGrammar`, `ContextFreeGrammar.Derives`.
(Proved here, following the cited route.)

### 4.3 Intersection with a regular language

Prove `isContextFree_inf`: if `L` is context-free and `R` is regular, `L ⊓ R` is context-free,
over any alphabet (Bar-Hillel, Perles and Shamir, 1961; Watrous, Theorem 9.3, p. 92, for finite
alphabets; Gallier–Quaintance, §7.16, p. 275, state the closure).

Route (Watrous, proof of Theorem 9.3): the empty word is handled apart, by 4.1 and the
membership of the empty word in `R`. For the rest take a grammar in Chomsky normal form for
`L - 1` (3.1) and an automaton for `R` on a finite `σ`. The new nonterminals are the triples
`(p, X, q)` with `p, q ∈ σ` and a fresh initial nonterminal; the rules are `(p, X, q) → a` when
`X → a` is a rule and `a` leads from `p` to `q`, and `(p, X, r) → (p, Y, q) (q, Z, r)` for each
rule `X → Y Z` and all `p, q, r`, and the initial nonterminal rewrites to `(start, S, f)` for each
accepting `f`. By induction on derivations, `(p, X, q)` derives the word `x` exactly when `X`
derives `x` and `x` leads from `p` to `q`. The rules are finitely many because the grammar's rules
and the states are; the alphabet may be infinite.

**Checks.**
- With the grammar `S → A X`, `S → A B`, `X → S B`, `A → a`, `B → b` for `{aⁿ bⁿ : n ≥ 1}` and the
  automata for the length congruent to 2 modulo 4, for the words with no factor `b a`, and for an
  even number of `a`, the triple grammar generates exactly the words of length at most 9 in the
  intersection; the first intersection is `{aⁿ bⁿ : n odd}`.
- The words over `{0, 1, 2}` with equally many of each letter form no context-free language: their
  intersection with the regular language of the words `0ⁱ 1ʲ 2ᵏ` is `equalBlocks`, which is not
  context-free (3.3).

*Needs:* 3.1, 4.1; Mathlib `DFA.evalFrom`, `DFA.evalFrom_of_append`, `ContextFreeGrammar`,
`ContextFreeGrammar.Derives`, `Language.isRegular_iff`. (Proved here, following the cited route.)

### 4.4 Intersection and complement

- `exists_not_isContextFree_inf`: there are context-free languages `L₁`, `L₂` over `Fin 3` whose
  intersection is not context-free.
- `exists_not_isContextFree_compl`: there is a context-free language over `Fin 3` whose complement
  is not context-free.

(Watrous, §10.3, p. 105.)

Route: `{0ⁿ 1ⁿ 2ᵐ} ⊓ {0ⁿ 1ᵐ 2ᵐ}` is `equalBlocks`, so 4.1 and 3.3 give the first statement. For
the second, with these `L₁` and `L₂`: if `L₁ᶜ` or `L₂ᶜ` is not context-free, that `L₁` or `L₂` is
the required language; otherwise `L₁ᶜ + L₂ᶜ` is context-free by 4.1, and its complement is
`L₁ ⊓ L₂`, which is not.

**Checks.**
- On all words over `{0, 1, 2}` of length at most 9, a word lies in both `{0ⁿ 1ⁿ 2ᵐ}` and
  `{0ⁿ 1ᵐ 2ᵐ}` exactly when it is some `0ⁿ 1ⁿ 2ⁿ`.
- Intersection with a regular language keeps the class (4.3), so neither `L₁` nor `L₂` in the
  first statement can be regular; indeed the intersection of `{0ⁿ 1ⁿ 2ᵐ}` with the regular
  language of the words `0ⁱ 1ʲ` is `{0ⁿ 1ⁿ}`, which is not regular (Check of 3.3), and likewise
  `{0ⁿ 1ᵐ 2ᵐ}` meets the words `1ʲ 2ᵏ` in `{1ᵐ 2ᵐ}`.

*Needs:* 3.3, 4.1. (Proved here, following the cited route.)

## Scope and ownership

This roadmap owns the targets stated above: closure of regular languages under concatenation and
star and both halves of Kleene's theorem; accessible, reduced and minimal automata, their
isomorphism with the automaton of left quotients, the existence and uniqueness of minimal automata
and their number of states; Chomsky normal form, the pumping lemma for context-free languages and
the language `0ⁿ 1ⁿ 2ⁿ`; closure of context-free languages under union, concatenation and star,
regular languages over a finite alphabet as context-free languages, intersection with a regular
language, and the failure of closure under intersection and complement.

Already in the library and therefore not targets: Mathlib's automata and their equivalences
(`NFA.toDFA_correct`, `εNFA.toNFA_correct`), closure of regular languages under complement, union,
intersection and reversal, the Myhill–Nerode theorem
(`Language.isRegular_iff_finite_range_leftQuotient`) with the automaton of left quotients
(`Language.toDFA`, `Language.accepts_toDFA`), the pumping lemma for regular languages
(`DFA.pumping_lemma`, `NFA.pumping_lemma`), the matching of regular expressions by derivatives
(`RegularExpression.rmatch_iff_matches'`), and context-free grammars with closure under reversal
(`Language.IsContextFree.reverse`).

No roadmap on main treats regular or context-free languages. ClassicalArithmeticCompletion, §2.8,
treats Tau Ceti's deterministic automata with output, for automatic sequences; nothing here uses
or restates them. ComputabilityTheory treats computably enumerable sets and does not meet the
targets above.

Outside the scope: pushdown automata and their equivalence with grammars, deterministic
context-free languages, Ogden's lemma beyond its unmarked case, Parikh's theorem, the
Chomsky–Schützenberger theorem, homomorphisms and inverse homomorphisms of languages, Büchi
automata and languages of infinite words, two-way and alternating automata, decision problems and
every conversion, minimization or parsing procedure as such.
